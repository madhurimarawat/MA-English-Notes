# Why We Hate Insects

**“Why We Hate Insects” by Robert Lynd** is a humorous but deeply thoughtful essay. It starts by discussing why humans dislike insects, but gradually becomes a discussion about **fear, prejudice, cruelty, and the danger of treating living beings as “vermin.”** The essay appears as Chapter X of Lynd’s *The Pleasures of Ignorance* (1921). 

## 1. Short Summary

Robert Lynd asks a simple question: **Why do human beings hate insects?**

He observes that our dislike is not always based on actual danger. For example, people may be terrified of a harmless spider but remain calm around a dangerous wasp or horsefly. Thus, **our fear of insects is often irrational rather than proportional to the real threat they pose.** 

Lynd discusses several insects:

* **Spiders** – often feared despite not necessarily being dangerous.
* **Fleas and lice** – hated because people associate them with dirt and disease.
* **Moths** – frightening to many people because of their mysterious movement at night, yet they can also be beautiful.
* **Flies** – considered annoying and dirty, but Lynd questions the cruelty with which humans sometimes kill them.
* **Bees and butterflies/ladybirds** – examples of insects that humans can admire because they appear beautiful or useful.

The essay's most important argument comes near the end. Humans call insects **“vermin”** and destroy them without much sympathy. Lynd warns that this attitude can also be applied to human beings. Once we begin to regard a person as an insect or vermin, **it becomes easier to treat that person without compassion.**

So the essay is ultimately **not merely about insects—it is about human prejudice and cruelty.**

# 2. Detailed Explanation

### A. Why do we hate insects?

Lynd begins by pointing out the strange contradiction that insects are an important part of nature, yet the very word **“insect”** often produces feelings of ugliness and disgust. 

He asks whether this hatred is:

* natural,
* inherited,
* or learned through society.

He suggests that much of our reaction is psychological.

### B. Fear does not equal danger

One of Lynd's most interesting observations is that **the insects we fear most are not necessarily the most dangerous.**

For example, a person might calmly watch a wasp but run away from a harmless spider.

Therefore:

> **“Our fears, it is evident, do not march in all respects with our sense of physical danger.”** 

This is one of the central ideas of the essay.

### C. The moth

Lynd gives special attention to moths.

He calls them:

> **“the ghosts of the insect world.”** 

Moths are interesting because they produce **both fear and fascination**. They appear suddenly from darkness and move unpredictably, which makes them seem mysterious.

At the same time, poetry has celebrated their beauty.

Thus, the moth represents the **dual nature of insects—beauty and terror.**

### D. Insects as destroyers

Lynd also explains why insects genuinely deserve some of our dislike.

They can:

* destroy crops,
* damage fruit,
* spread disease,
* irritate humans,
* suck blood,
* and cause suffering to humans and animals.

He compares the destructive activities of insects with **spies**, because both seem to enter places where they are not wanted and cause harm.

### E. The deeper moral argument

This is the most important part for exams.

Humans call insects **“vermin”** and then feel justified in killing them.

But Lynd warns that the same mentality can be applied to human beings.

His argument is essentially:

**Insect → vermin → something without value → something we can destroy without remorse.**

If human beings are also described as vermin or insects, violence against them can become psychologically easier to justify.

That is why Lynd says:

> **“To regard a human being as an insect is always the first step in treating him without remorse.”**

This transforms the essay from a humorous nature essay into a **serious moral criticism of dehumanization.**

# 3. Main Themes

### 1. Fear and Irrationality

Humans do not always fear insects according to the actual danger they present.

**Example:** A harmless spider may frighten someone more than a dangerous wasp.

**Theme:** Human fear is often psychological rather than rational.

---

### 2. Beauty vs. Ugliness

Some insects are considered beautiful while others are considered disgusting.

Lynd particularly contrasts:

* butterflies/ladybirds/bees → beauty
* spiders/fleas/flies → disgust

This shows that our judgment of nature is often based on **appearance rather than objective value.**

---

### 3. Human Prejudice

Humans classify creatures as:

* beautiful,
* ugly,
* useful,
* useless,
* dangerous,
* harmless.

Lynd suggests that this habit of categorizing can become dangerous when applied to human beings.

---

### 4. Cruelty

The essay questions the casual cruelty with which people kill insects.

Lynd especially dislikes unnecessarily cruel methods of killing flies. He argues that even if we must kill an insect, we should not take pleasure in its suffering.

---

### 5. Dehumanization

This is probably the **most important theme**.

Calling someone an insect or “vermin” removes their individuality and makes cruelty seem acceptable.

Therefore, the essay becomes a warning about **how language can prepare people for violence.**

---

### 6. Human Relationship with Nature

Lynd reminds us that insects are part of the natural world. We cannot simply judge nature according to whether it pleases us.

The essay encourages greater **understanding and sympathy toward living creatures.**

# 4. Important Quotes for Exams

These are the **best quotes to remember**:

### Quote 1 — Human fear

> **“Our fears, it is evident, do not march in all respects with our sense of physical danger.”** 

**Meaning:** What we fear is not always what is actually dangerous.

**Use for:** Fear, irrationality, psychology.

---

### Quote 2 — Moths

> **“Moths are the ghosts of the insect world.”** 

**Meaning:** Moths seem mysterious and frightening because of their nighttime movements.

**Use for:** Symbolism, beauty vs terror.

---

### Quote 3 — Insects and spies

> **“We regard them as noxious creatures intruding where they have no right to be.”**

**Meaning:** Humans hate insects partly because they see them as unwanted intruders.

**Use for:** Human attitude toward insects.

---

### Quote 4 — Dehumanization ⭐

> **“To regard a human being as an insect is always the first step in treating him without remorse.”**

**Meaning:** When we stop seeing someone as a human being, cruelty toward them becomes easier.

**Use for:** Main theme, morality, prejudice, dehumanization.

---

### Quote 5 — Ruthlessness

> **“Hence our ruthlessness. We say: ‘Vermin,’ and destroy them.”**

**Meaning:** Calling insects “vermin” makes us feel justified in destroying them.

**Use for:** Cruelty and language.

# 6. Why is the title important?

**“Why We Hate Insects”** sounds like the essay will simply explain why insects are unpleasant.

But Lynd uses the title ironically.

The real question becomes:

**Why do human beings develop hatred toward creatures—and why do we sometimes extend the same hatred and cruelty to other human beings?**

So the essay moves:

**Insects → Fear → Prejudice → Cruelty → Dehumanization → Human morality**

That is the key progression to remember.

# 7. One-paragraph Summary

**Robert Lynd's “Why We Hate Insects” is a humorous yet philosophical essay examining the human dislike and fear of insects. Lynd points out that our fears are often irrational because we sometimes fear harmless insects while ignoring more dangerous ones. He discusses spiders, fleas, flies and moths and shows how ideas of ugliness, dirt, danger and mystery influence our attitudes. The essay gradually develops into a moral argument against cruelty and prejudice. Lynd's most important point is that calling insects “vermin” makes it easier to destroy them without remorse, and the same process of dehumanization can be applied to human beings. Thus, the essay is not merely about insects; it explores fear, prejudice, cruelty, beauty, human psychology and the dangers of treating others as less than human.**
