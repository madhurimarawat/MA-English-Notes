# Selected Classical Poets and Writers

## Greek

### Poets

### 1. 🌸 Sappho — famous poems/fragments

Sappho (c. 630–570 BCE) is famous especially for **lyric poetry, love, desire, and personal emotion**.

1. **“Ode to Aphrodite”** (*Hymn to Aphrodite*) — her most famous surviving complete poem.

   * She appeals to Aphrodite to help her in love.
   * Important for its intensely personal voice and treatment of female desire.

2. **“He seems to me equal to the gods”** (Fragment 31)

   * Describes Sappho's physical and emotional reaction while watching the woman she desires speak to another man.
   * Famous for its depiction of **jealousy, desire, and bodily sensation**.

3. **“Midnight Poem”** (Fragment 48/various fragment numbering)

   * Concerns longing and separation.

4. **“The Tithonus Poem”** (Fragment 58)

   * Reflects on **aging, beauty, and mortality**.

5. **“Brothers Poem”** (Fragment 17)

   * Concerns her brothers and family.

**Key themes:** love, female desire, beauty, jealousy, separation, aging, mortality, nature.

### 2. 🏛️ Pindar — famous poems

**Pindar** (c. 518–438 BCE) is best known for his **victory odes**, written to celebrate winners of the major Greek athletic games.

His surviving *Epinician Odes* are grouped according to the games:

1. **Olympian Odes**

   * *Olympian 1* — celebrates **Hieron of Syracuse's horse-racing victory**.
   * *Olympian 2* — celebrates Theron of Acragas.
   * *Olympian 3* — celebrates Theron's victory.

2. **Pythian Odes**

   * **Pythian 1** — one of his most famous odes; celebrates Hieron's victory and contains the famous myth of **Apollo and the founding of Delphi**.
   * **Pythian 8** — famous for its reflections on **human mortality and uncertainty**.

3. **Nemean Odes**

   * Celebrates winners of the Nemean Games.

4. **Isthmian Odes**

   * Celebrates winners of the Isthmian Games.

**Key themes:** athletic victory, glory (*kleos*), gods and heroes, mythology, human mortality, fame, virtue, and the relationship between human achievement and divine power.

### 📚 Summary

| Poet       | Famous work        | Main type   | Main concern                |
| ---------- | ------------------ | ----------- | --------------------------- |
| **Sappho** | *Ode to Aphrodite* | Lyric       | Love & desire               |
| **Sappho** | Fragment 31        | Lyric       | Jealousy & physical emotion |
| **Pindar** | *Olympian 1*       | Victory ode | Athletic glory & mythology  |
| **Pindar** | *Pythian 1*        | Victory ode | Victory, gods & myth        |
| **Pindar** | *Pythian 8*        | Victory ode | Mortality & uncertainty     |

**Easy memory trick:** **Sappho = personal love lyric; Pindar = public victory ode.**

### Writers

Socrates did **not write any books or create a philosophical system in the way Plato or Aristotle did**. He is mainly known through the writings of his students and contemporaries, especially **Plato and Xenophon**.

### 🏛️ Socrates — famous contributions

1. **Socratic Method**

   * His most famous contribution.
   * Uses **questions and answers** to examine someone's beliefs and expose contradictions.
   * Also called the **elenchus** or Socratic questioning.

2. **“Know Thyself”**

   * Socrates strongly emphasized self-examination and awareness of one's own ignorance.
   * Closely associated with the Delphic maxim **“Know thyself.”**

3. **The examined life**

   * His philosophy emphasizes that people should critically examine their beliefs and actions.
   * Famous statement from Plato's *Apology*: **“The unexamined life is not worth living.”**

4. **Socratic irony**

   * Socrates often presented himself as someone who did not know the answer, then questioned another person until weaknesses in their knowledge became apparent.

5. **Ethics and virtue**

   * He shifted much philosophical attention toward questions such as:

     * What is justice?
     * What is virtue?
     * What is courage?
     * What is the good life?
   * He is associated with the idea that **knowledge and virtue are closely connected**.

### 📚 Important works *about* Socrates

Since Socrates himself wrote nothing, these are especially important:

* **Plato — *Apology*** → Socrates' defense at his trial.
* **Plato — *Crito*** → Socrates discusses justice and whether he should escape prison.
* **Plato — *Phaedo*** → His final hours and death.
* **Plato — *Symposium*** → Socrates and the nature of love.
* **Plato — *Republic*** → Socrates is the principal speaker discussing justice and the ideal state.
* **Xenophon — *Memorabilia*** → recollections and defense of Socrates.
* **Xenophon — *Apology of Socrates*** → Socrates' trial and defense.

### 📝 Shortcut

**Socrates → Socratic Method → questioning → self-knowledge → virtue → examined life → no writings of his own → known mainly through Plato and Xenophon.**

## Roman

### Poets

**Horace wrote *Ars Poetica***, also known as **“The Art of Poetry.”**

### Horace — *Ars Poetica*

* **Date:** c. **19–18 BCE** (usually dated around 20 BCE)
* **Form:** A **verse epistle** written to the **Piso family**, so it is also called *Epistle to the Pisos*.
* **Subject:** **Poetry and dramatic composition**
* **Influence:** One of the most influential works of **classical literary criticism**.

### ⭐ Important ideas in *Ars Poetica*

1. **Unity and coherence** — a literary work should have its parts properly connected.
2. **Decorum** — style and language should suit the **character, subject, age, and situation**.
3. **Dramatic structure** — especially important for tragedy.
4. **Character consistency** — characters should behave according to their established nature.
5. **Dramatic economy** — avoid unnecessary details and overly complicated plots.
6. **Purpose of poetry** — Horace's famous principle is **“dulce et utile”**:

   * **dulce** = to delight
   * **utile** = to instruct/usefully teach

### 📝 Exam connection

**Horace → *Ars Poetica* → “Art of Poetry” → literary criticism → decorum → unity → “dulce et utile.”**

## Dramatists

Yes — if you are preparing **Ancient Roman drama**, apart from **Plautus, Terence, and Seneca**, there are several other dramatists and dramatic writers worth knowing. The important thing is to distinguish **Old/Republican Roman drama** from the **Imperial period**.

### 🎭 The important periods

#### 1. Early Roman / Republican Drama

**3rd–2nd century BCE**

The Romans initially borrowed heavily from **Greek drama**, especially Greek **New Comedy** and Greek tragedy.

**Livius Andronicus → Naevius → Ennius → Plautus → Terence**

For exams, this sequence is useful.

#### 2. Late Republican Period

**2nd–1st century BCE**

Important tragedians:

* **Pacuvius**
* **Accius**

Important comic tradition:

* **Caecilius Statius**
* **Afranius**

**Accius** is particularly important as the **last major Republican tragedian before Seneca**.

#### 3. Imperial Roman Period

**1st century CE onward**

The major surviving Roman tragedian is:

**Seneca → *Phaedra*, *Medea*, *Thyestes*, *Oedipus*, *Agamemnon*, *Troades*, *Hercules Furens*, *Hercules Oetaeus*, *Phoenissae*, *Octavia*.**

## ⭐ Very important for English Literature exams

You can remember Roman drama through this simplified chain:

**Livius Andronicus** → beginning of Roman literary drama
↓
**Naevius** → tragedy + Roman historical themes
↓
**Ennius** → major early Roman tragedian + *Annales*
↓
**Plautus** → Roman comedy
↓
**Terence** → sophisticated Roman comedy
↓
**Pacuvius** → major tragedian
↓
**Accius** → last great Republican tragedian
↓
**Seneca** → Imperial Roman tragedy

### One particularly important non-dramatist

**Horace's *Ars Poetica*** is worth remembering even though Horace was not primarily a dramatist. It discusses **poetry, drama, plot, character, decorum, style, and the proper construction of tragedy** and later became enormously influential in European literary criticism.

**Exam keywords:**
**Roman comedy → Plautus, Terence**
**Republican tragedy → Ennius, Pacuvius, Accius**
**Early Roman drama → Livius Andronicus, Naevius**
**Imperial tragedy → Seneca**
**Mime → Laberius, Publilius Syrus**
**Dramatic criticism → Horace, *Ars Poetica***
