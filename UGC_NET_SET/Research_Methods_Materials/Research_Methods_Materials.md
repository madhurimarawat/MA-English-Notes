---

# 🧠 1. WHAT IS RESEARCH?

The word **research** can be understood as:

> **Re + Search = searching again / systematic investigation**

Research is a:

> **systematic, organized, critical and evidence-based investigation undertaken to discover, interpret or establish knowledge.**

In English Literature, research may investigate:

* an author
* a literary text
* a genre
* a historical period
* a literary movement
* a theory
* language
* culture
* manuscripts
* reception
* translation
* publishing
* readership
* digital literature

---

# 🎯 SIMPLE EXAMPLE

Suppose our topic is:

> **“Women and Domestic Space in Jane Austen's Novels.”**

We don't simply read Austen and give an opinion.

We must:

1. identify a research problem
2. formulate questions
3. review previous scholarship
4. select texts
5. select a theoretical framework
6. collect evidence
7. analyze it
8. cite our sources
9. draw conclusions

That entire systematic process = **research**.

---

# 🔥 GOLDEN RESEARCH CHAIN

Memorize:

> **Topic → Problem → Questions → Review → Hypothesis → Method → Materials → Data → Analysis → Argument → Conclusion → Documentation**

Not every humanities project uses every stage in exactly this order, but this is an excellent **NET revision framework**.

---

# 2. RESEARCH vs CASUAL READING

| Casual reading                    | Research                 |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Personal interest                 | Systematic investigation |
| May be unsystematic               | Planned                  |
| Opinion may be enough             | Evidence required        |
| No formal documentation necessary | Documentation essential  |
| No research question              | Research question        |
| No literature review              | Literature review        |
| Personal conclusion               | Evidence-based argument  |

### 🧠 NET

Research is **not simply collecting information**.

Research must involve:

> **a question + method + evidence + analysis + argument.**

---

# 3. PURPOSES OF RESEARCH

Research can be conducted to:

### 🔍 Discover

Find something not previously known.

### 📖 Describe

Provide a systematic account.

### 🧠 Explain

Explain relationships or causes.

### 🔬 Analyze

Break a subject into meaningful parts.

### ⚖️ Evaluate

Assess competing interpretations.

### 🔄 Interpret

Develop a reading of a text or phenomenon.

### 🧩 Compare

Compare authors, texts, periods, cultures, etc.

### 🏗️ Develop theory

Build or refine theoretical understanding.

---

# 4. TYPES OF RESEARCH

This is extremely important for NET.

There are several ways to classify research.

---

# 🟢 A. BASIC / PURE RESEARCH

Also called:

> **Fundamental research**

Purpose:

> To increase knowledge and theoretical understanding.

It may not have an immediate practical application.

### Example

Studying:

> **Narrative time in postmodern fiction**

without an immediate practical goal.

---

# 🟡 B. APPLIED RESEARCH

Purpose:

> Solve a specific practical problem.

Example:

> Investigating methods for improving English-language teaching in rural colleges.

---

# 🧠 BASIC vs APPLIED

**Basic = knowledge**

**Applied = solution**

### Memory:

> 🧠 **Basic = “Why/What?”**
> 🛠️ **Applied = “How can we use it?”**

---

# 🔵 C. EXPLORATORY RESEARCH

Used when a subject is not sufficiently understood.

Purpose:

> Explore a problem and generate ideas/questions.

Often relatively flexible.

Example:

> Exploring how students use AI tools for literary research.

---

# 🟣 D. DESCRIPTIVE RESEARCH

Answers:

> **What is happening?**

It describes characteristics of a phenomenon.

Example:

> A study of reading habits among postgraduate English students.

---

# 🔴 E. EXPLANATORY RESEARCH

Answers:

> **Why does it happen?**

It attempts to explain relationships and causes.

---

# 🟠 F. ANALYTICAL RESEARCH

The researcher:

> examines and interprets existing information.

Very common in humanities.

Example:

> Analyzing representations of caste in selected novels.

---

# 🟤 G. HISTORICAL RESEARCH

Studies:

> past events, texts, institutions, authors, movements and contexts.

Literary example:

> Development of Indian English poetry between 1850 and 1950.

Materials may include:

* manuscripts
* letters
* diaries
* newspapers
* archival documents
* first editions
* historical records

---

# 🟧 H. COMPARATIVE RESEARCH

Compares:

* authors
* texts
* genres
* cultures
* periods
* theories
* languages

Example:

> Comparing the representation of exile in Rushdie and Lahiri.

---

# 🟦 I. QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

This is VERY important.

Qualitative research focuses on:

> **meaning, interpretation, experience, language, culture and context.**

Common materials:

* interviews
* observations
* textual analysis
* diaries
* narratives
* archival material
* discourse

### Literature example

Analyzing:

> representation of trauma in Partition literature.

we examine language, imagery, narrative, memory, etc.

---

# 🟥 J. QUANTITATIVE RESEARCH

Uses:

> numerical data and statistical analysis.

Example:

> Surveying 500 students about their use of digital literary databases.

we may use:

* percentages
* frequencies
* averages
* correlations
* statistical tests

---

# 🧠 QUALITATIVE vs QUANTITATIVE

| Qualitative               | Quantitative                 |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Words                     | Numbers                      |
| Meaning                   | Measurement                  |
| Interpretation            | Statistical analysis         |
| Interviews                | Surveys                      |
| Textual analysis          | Numerical data               |
| Context                   | Variables                    |
| Smaller/deeper data often | Larger/measurable data often |

### Memory:

> 🗣️ **Qualitative = quality/meaning**

> 🔢 **Quantitative = quantity/numbers**

---

# 🟪 K. MIXED-METHODS RESEARCH

Combines:

> **qualitative + quantitative**

Example:

we study digital reading habits.

### Quantitative:

Survey 1,000 students.

### Qualitative:

Interview 30 students about their reading experiences.

Together:

> **Mixed methods**

---

# 5. EMPIRICAL RESEARCH

Empirical research is based on:

> **observation or experience and systematically gathered evidence.**

It may use:

* observation
* interviews
* surveys
* experiments
* fieldwork

### Important distinction

Literary research can be highly interpretive, while empirical research typically emphasizes observable or collected evidence.

---

# 6. CONCEPTUAL RESEARCH

Based primarily on:

> concepts, theories, models and intellectual analysis.

Example:

> A study comparing Derrida's concept of différance with Deleuze's theory of difference.

No laboratory experiment is required.

---

# 7. THEORETICAL RESEARCH

Examines:

* theories
* concepts
* philosophical arguments
* models
* critical frameworks

Example:

> Reconsidering the concept of the “author” through Barthes and Foucault.

---

# 8. DOCTRINAL vs NON-DOCTRINAL

More common in legal/social research but sometimes useful for general research-method questions.

### Doctrinal

Studies existing:

* texts
* rules
* principles
* documents

### Non-doctrinal

Uses empirical data such as:

* interviews
* surveys
* observations

---

# 📚 9. LITERARY RESEARCH

For English Literature, research commonly involves:

### Textual research

Close reading of literary texts.

### Historical research

Studying literature within historical context.

### Biographical research

Author's life and literary production.

### Comparative research

Comparing texts/authors/traditions.

### Theoretical research

Using literary theory.

### Archival research

Manuscripts, letters, editions, documents.

### Reception research

Studying how readers/critics responded to a text.

### Digital humanities research

Using digital tools and databases.

# 🎯 11. RESEARCH PROBLEM

A research problem is:

> **a specific issue, gap, contradiction or question that requires systematic investigation.**

Bad topic:

> “Women in Literature”

Too broad.

Better:

> “Domestic confinement and female agency in selected novels of Anita Desai.”

Even better:

> “How does domestic space function as both confinement and agency in selected novels of Anita Desai?”

---

# ❓ 12. RESEARCH QUESTION

A research question is the specific question our study attempts to answer.

Examples:

> How does memory shape identity in *The Shadow Lines*?

> How is the female body represented in Kamala Das's poetry?

> How does Bhabha's concept of hybridity illuminate *Midnight's Children*?

---

# 🧠 GOOD RESEARCH QUESTION

Usually:

* focused
* clear
* researchable
* not merely factual
* connected to evidence
* neither excessively broad nor impossibly narrow

---

# 13. HYPOTHESIS ⭐

A hypothesis is:

> **a tentative statement or prediction that can be investigated.**

Example:

> “The selected novels represent migration not merely as geographical movement but as a transformation of cultural identity.”

The researcher then investigates whether evidence supports this claim.

---

# 🚨 LITERARY RESEARCH TRAP

Not every humanities research project requires a formal hypothesis.

Literary studies often use:

> **research questions + thesis/argument**

rather than a testable hypothesis in the experimental-science sense.

---

# 14. THESIS STATEMENT

A thesis statement is:

> **the central argument of the research.**

Example:

> “In *The Shadow Lines*, borders are represented as psychological and ideological constructions rather than merely geographical boundaries.”

### Difference:

**Research question**

> What does the novel say about borders?

**Thesis**

> The novel presents borders as psychological and ideological constructions.

---

# 📖 15. LITERATURE REVIEW ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

One of the most important research components.

A literature review is:

> **a critical and organized discussion of existing scholarship related to our research topic.**

It is NOT:

❌ simply a list of books.

It should identify:

* major arguments
* important scholars
* theoretical approaches
* disagreements
* gaps
* developments
* areas requiring further research

---

# 🧠 LITERATURE REVIEW =

> **What has already been said + what has not been said + where my research enters.**

---

# 🔍 16. RESEARCH GAP

A research gap means:

> **an area insufficiently studied, unresolved, overlooked, or open to further investigation.**

Example:

Many scholars study:

> colonialism in Indian English fiction.

But relatively few study:

> ecological memory in a specific group of contemporary Indian novels.

That unexplored area may become a research gap.

---

# 🚨 NET TRAP

### Literature review

❌ summary of every book we read

✅ critical engagement with existing scholarship

---

# 📚 17. SOURCES / RESEARCH MATERIALS

This is VERY important.

Research materials are often divided into:

# PRIMARY SOURCES

and

# SECONDARY SOURCES

---

# 🟢 PRIMARY SOURCE

A primary source is:

> **direct/original material that constitutes the object or immediate evidence of study.**

For literary research:

* original novel
* poem
* play
* manuscript
* author's letters
* diary
* first edition
* interview with author
* archival document
* original newspaper article
* original historical document

### Example

If studying:

> *The God of Small Things*

The novel itself is our:

🟢 **primary source**

---

# 🔵 SECONDARY SOURCE

Secondary sources:

> **analyze, interpret, critique or discuss primary sources.**

Examples:

* journal articles
* critical books
* scholarly essays
* biographies
* dissertations
* literary criticism
* theoretical studies

Example:

An article discussing:

> caste in *The God of Small Things*

is a:

🔵 **secondary source**

---

# 🧠 EASY MEMORY

> **PRIMARY = the thing we study**

> **SECONDARY = what scholars say about the thing**

---

# 🟣 TERTIARY SOURCES

Tertiary sources organize or summarize existing knowledge.

Examples:

* encyclopedias
* dictionaries
* indexes
* bibliographies
* some handbooks
* reference databases

They are useful for:

> orientation and locating sources.

But they should not automatically replace primary/secondary scholarly sources.

Original handwritten or typed document.

### First edition

First published edition of a work.

### Later edition

A subsequent publication.

### Critical edition

# ✍️ 21. TEXTUAL CRITICISM

Very important for literary research.

Textual criticism attempts to:

> **establish or reconstruct the most reliable text from available textual witnesses.**

Suppose a novel exists in:

* manuscript A
* manuscript B
* first edition
* revised edition

There are differences.

A textual scholar compares them.

This is:

> **textual criticism**

---

# 🧠 TEXTUAL CRITICISM vs LITERARY CRITICISM

### Textual criticism

> What is the most reliable version of the text?

### Literary criticism

> What does the text mean/how should it be interpreted?

---

# 📚 22. CLOSE READING

One of the most fundamental methods in literary studies.

Close reading examines:

* diction
* syntax
* imagery
* metaphor
* symbolism
* narrative
* form
* structure
* tone
* repetition
* sound
* irony

It asks:

> **How does the text produce meaning?**

---

# 🔬 23. NEW CRITICISM AND CLOSE READING

New Criticism strongly emphasized:

> careful attention to the text itself.

Important names:

* I.A. Richards
* John Crowe Ransom
* Cleanth Brooks
* W.K. Wimsatt
* Monroe Beardsley

Key concepts:

### Intentional Fallacy

Wimsatt & Beardsley

→ author's intention should not determine interpretation.

### Affective Fallacy

→ reader's emotional response should not be treated as the final measure of textual value.

Systematic examination of content.

Can be:

* qualitative

> “mother”

# 📊 25. DISCOURSE ANALYSIS

Studies:

> language in relation to power, social practices and systems of meaning.

> How colonial newspapers represented Indians.

> systems of language, knowledge and practices through which objects/subjects are represented and understood.

# 🚨 49. PLAGIARISM ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

One of the most important NET topics.

Plagiarism means:

> **presenting another person's words, ideas, data or intellectual work as our own without appropriate acknowledgment.**

---

# TYPES OF PLAGIARISM

### 1. Direct plagiarism

Copying exact words without citation.

### 2. Mosaic / patchwork plagiarism

Taking phrases or structures from multiple sources and presenting them as original.

### 3. Paraphrasing plagiarism

Changing a few words but keeping the original structure/idea without acknowledgment.

### 4. Self-plagiarism

Reusing one's own previously published/submitted material without appropriate disclosure.

### 5. Accidental plagiarism

Failure to cite due to carelessness.

Even if accidental, it can still be academically problematic.

---

# 🧠 HOW TO AVOID PLAGIARISM

### Quote

Use exact words + citation.

### Paraphrase

Rewrite genuinely in our own structure + citation.

### Summarize

Condense the argument + citation.

### Cite

Give proper source information.

---

# 🚨 IMPORTANT

Changing a few words is **NOT** proper paraphrasing.

Real paraphrasing means:

> **understanding → rethinking → rewriting → citing**

---

# 📌 50. DIRECT QUOTATION

Uses the author's exact words.

Needs:

* quotation marks or block quotation depending on length/style
* citation

---

# 🔄 51. PARAPHRASE

Expresses the source's idea in our own words.

Still:

> **requires citation.**

---

# 📋 52. SUMMARY

Shortens the main argument.

Still:

> **requires citation if derived from a source.**

---

# 🧠 QUOTE vs PARAPHRASE vs SUMMARY

| Method     | Meaning                 | Citation? |
| ---------- | ----------------------- | --------- |
| Quote      | Exact words             | ✅         |
| Paraphrase | Same idea, our wording | ✅         |
| Summary    | Condensed argument      | ✅         |

# 📚 53. MLA STYLE ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

For UGC-NET English, **MLA is especially important**.

MLA =

> **Modern Language Association**

Commonly used in:

* literature
* language
* humanities

Current MLA Handbook:

> **9th edition — 2021**

---

# ✍️ MLA IN-TEXT CITATION

Basic form:

> **(Author page)**

Example:

> (Eagleton 15)

No comma between author and page in standard MLA parenthetical citation.

---

# 📖 MLA WORKS CITED

Basic book format:

> Author. *Title*. Publisher, Year.

Example structure:

> Eagleton, Terry. *Literary Theory: An Introduction*. University of Minnesota Press, 1996.

---

# 🌐 MLA WEBSITE / ONLINE SOURCE

Modern MLA emphasizes:

> **core elements**

such as:

* author
* title
* container
* contributors
* version
* number
* publisher
* publication date
* location

The important principle is:

> **Identify the source clearly enough for the reader to locate it.**

---

# 📚 54. APA STYLE

APA =

> **American Psychological Association**

Common in:

* psychology
* education
* social sciences

APA emphasizes:

> **author + date**

Example:

> (Smith, 2020)

---

# 📖 MLA vs APA

| MLA         | APA                                 |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Humanities  | Social sciences/behavioral sciences |
| Author-page | Author-date                         |
| Works Cited | References                          |
| Literature  | Psychology/education etc.           |

---

* history

> parenthetical author-date citations + reference list

A footnote provides:

> citation or additional information at the bottom of the page.

> 🦶 Footnote = bottom of page

📘 Encyclopedia entry on Indian English literature

# 🔬 64. VALIDITY

Validity asks:

> **Does the research method actually measure/investigate what it claims to measure/investigate?**

Example:

If we claim to study:

> students' reading habits

but only ask them about their favorite authors, our instrument may not fully capture reading habits.

---

# 🎯 65. RELIABILITY

Reliability means:

> **consistency of measurement.**

If a method produces similar results under similar conditions, it is more reliable.

### Memory

> **Validity = accuracy**

> **Reliability = consistency**

---

# 🚨 IMPORTANT

A method can be:

> reliable but not valid.

Example:

A broken weighing scale always shows 5 kg too much.

It is consistent:

✅ reliable

But inaccurate:

❌ not valid

---

# 🎯 66. OBJECTIVITY

Objectivity means attempting to minimize:

> personal bias and subjective distortion.

But in humanities, complete neutrality is often debated.

Interpretive research recognizes that:

> researcher position and perspective can influence interpretation.

---

# 🪞 67. SUBJECTIVITY

Subjectivity refers to:

> the influence of the researcher's perspective, experience, assumptions or position.

Modern qualitative research may explicitly acknowledge:

> **researcher positionality**

---

# 📍 68. POSITIONALITY

Researcher positionality asks:

> How does the researcher's social, cultural, intellectual or institutional position influence the research?

Especially important in:

* feminist research
* postcolonial research
* ethnography
* qualitative research

---

# ⚖️ 69. RESEARCH ETHICS ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Research ethics includes:

### Honesty

Don't fabricate data.

### Accuracy

Don't manipulate findings.

### Proper citation

Acknowledge sources.

### Consent

Participants should generally know what participation involves.

### Confidentiality

Protect participant information when required.

### Avoid harm

Research should not unnecessarily harm participants.

### Transparency

Explain methods and limitations honestly.

---

# 🚨 70. FABRICATION vs FALSIFICATION

Very common research ethics question.

### Fabrication

> **Making up data/results that never existed.**

### Falsification

> **Manipulating or altering data/materials/results.**

### Plagiarism

> **Using someone else's work without proper acknowledgment.**

### 🧠 MEMORY

> **Fabrication = CREATE fake data**

> **Falsification = CHANGE real data**

> **Plagiarism = STEAL intellectual work**

---

# 🔐 71. INFORMED CONSENT

Participants should generally receive sufficient information about:

* purpose
* procedures
* risks
* benefits
* participation
* withdrawal

before agreeing to participate.

---

# 🔒 72. CONFIDENTIALITY vs ANONYMITY

### Confidentiality

Researcher knows identity but protects it.

### Anonymity

Identity is not known/recorded or is not linked to the data, depending on the research design.

### Memory:

> **Confidential = researcher may know**

> **Anonymous = identity not connected/known**

---

# 🧠 73. VARIABLES

Especially important in quantitative research.

A variable is:

> a characteristic that can take different values.

---

# 🟢 INDEPENDENT VARIABLE

The factor that is manipulated or treated as the predictor.

### Example

Teaching method.

---

# 🔵 DEPENDENT VARIABLE

The outcome being measured.

### Example

Student performance.

### Memory

> **Independent → influences**

> **Dependent → responds**

---

# 🧪 74. CONTROL VARIABLE

A variable held constant or accounted for so that its influence does not distort the analysis.

---

# 📊 75. POPULATION

The complete group relevant to a study.

Example:

> All postgraduate English students in a university.

---

# 👥 76. SAMPLE

A smaller group selected from the population.

Example:

> 200 postgraduate English students.

### Memory

> **Population = whole**

> **Sample = part**

---

# 🎯 77. SAMPLING

Sampling is:

> selecting participants/items from a population.

---

# 🟢 PROBABILITY SAMPLING

Every member has a known/non-zero probability of selection, depending on the design.

Types:

### Simple random sampling

Everyone has an equal chance under the standard design.

### Systematic sampling

Select every nth item after a starting point.

### Stratified sampling

Divide population into groups/strata and sample from them.

### Cluster sampling

Select groups/clusters rather than individuals directly.

---

# 🟡 NON-PROBABILITY SAMPLING

Selection is not based on known random probabilities.

Types:

### Convenience sampling

Choose easily available participants.

### Purposive sampling

Choose participants because they fit the research purpose.

### Snowball sampling

Participants help recruit other participants.

Useful for hard-to-reach populations.

---

# 🧠 78. CORRELATION

Correlation measures:

> **association between variables.**

Important:

> **Correlation does not automatically mean causation.**

🚨 Very common research-method trap.

---

# 📈 79. CAUSATION

Causation means:

> change in one factor contributes to producing change in another under appropriate conditions.

Do NOT automatically infer causation from correlation.

---

# 🧠 80. DEDUCTIVE vs INDUCTIVE REASONING

### Deductive

Moves from:

> **general → specific**

Example:

General theory → test against specific evidence.

### Inductive

Moves from:

> **specific → general**

Example:

Observe multiple cases → develop a broader conclusion.

### Memory

> 🔽 **Deduction = Down from general**

> 🔼 **Induction = Build generalization from instances**

---

# 🔄 81. ABDUCTIVE REASONING

Moves toward:

> the best available explanation for observed evidence.

Useful when researchers encounter unexpected findings.

---

# 🧠 82. THEORY

A theory is:

> a systematic set of concepts/propositions used to explain or interpret phenomena.

Examples in literary research:

* Marxism
* Feminism
* Psychoanalysis
* Structuralism
* Poststructuralism
* Postcolonialism
* New Historicism
* Ecocriticism

---

# 🧩 83. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

The theoretical framework explains:

> **which theory/theories guide our interpretation and why.**

Example:

Research topic:

> Migration in Indian English fiction

Theoretical framework:

> Bhabha's hybridity + diaspora theory

---

# 🧰 84. METHODOLOGY vs METHOD ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Very important.

### METHOD

The specific technique used.

Examples:

* interview
* survey
* close reading
* archival analysis

### METHODOLOGY

The broader rationale and systematic approach explaining:

> **why these methods are appropriate and how the research is designed.**

### 🧠 MEMORY

> **Method = What I do**

> **Methodology = Why/how I do it**

---

# 🚨 NET TRAP

Do not treat:

> method = methodology

as exact synonyms in research-method discussions.

---

# 📚 85. RESEARCH METHOD vs RESEARCH MATERIAL

### Method

How we investigate.

### Material

What we investigate/use.

Example:

> **Method:** close reading

> **Material:** *The God of Small Things*

# 📖 92. PEER REVIEW

Peer review means:

> scholarly work is evaluated by experts in the relevant field before publication, according to the journal/publisher's process.

Purpose:

* assess quality
* identify errors
* evaluate argument/method
* improve scholarship

---

# ⚠️ 93. PREDATORY JOURNALS

Predatory publishers may:

* charge fees
* provide inadequate peer review
* make misleading claims
* prioritize publication over scholarly quality

Researchers should evaluate journals carefully.

---

# 🧠 94. RESEARCH BIAS

Bias can enter through:

* research question
* sampling
* source selection
* interpretation
* data analysis
* publication

Researchers should:

> identify and minimize unjustified bias.

---

# 🔬 95. TRIANGULATION

Important qualitative research concept.

Triangulation means:

> **using multiple sources, methods, theories or researchers to strengthen understanding of a research problem.**

Example:

Study a cultural phenomenon using:

* interviews
* observations
* documents

If findings converge, confidence in the interpretation may increase.

---

# 🔄 96. REPLICATION

Replication means:

> conducting a study again to see whether findings can be reproduced or supported.

More central in empirical/scientific research, but the concept is broadly important.

---

# 📊 97. PEER REVIEW vs PLAGIARISM

Don't confuse:

### Peer review

Quality-control process.

### Plagiarism

Academic misconduct involving unattributed use of others' work.

---

# 📑 98. APPENDIX / APPENDICES

An appendix contains supplementary material that supports the research but is too detailed for the main discussion.

Examples:

* questionnaire
* interview questions
* large tables
* archival images
* transcripts
* additional data

---

# 📚 99. ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

Recognizes people/institutions that helped the research.

Examples:

* supervisor
* funding agency
* archive
* participants
* institution

It is not a substitute for formal citation.

---

# 🧠 100. RESEARCH PROPOSAL

A research proposal explains:

> **what we want to research, why it matters, and how we plan to do it.**

Usually includes:

1. Title
2. Background
3. Research problem
4. Research questions
5. Objectives
6. Literature review
7. Research gap
8. Theoretical framework
9. Methodology
10. Sources/materials
11. Expected contribution
12. Tentative chapter plan
13. Bibliography

---

# 🎯 101. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

Objectives describe:

> **what the research intends to accomplish.**

Good objectives are:

* clear
* specific
* achievable
* connected to the research problem.

Example:

> To examine representations of migration in selected Indian English novels.

---

# ❓ 102. RESEARCH QUESTIONS vs OBJECTIVES

### Research question

> What do I want to find out?

### Objective

> What will I do to investigate it?

---

# 🧠 103. LIMITATIONS

Every research project has limitations.

Examples:

* limited sample
* limited archive access
* restricted time
* limited number of texts
* language barriers
* incomplete historical records

Acknowledging limitations is:

> **good scholarship**, not weakness.

---

# 📝 104. CONCLUSION

A conclusion should:

* return to research questions
* summarize major findings
* explain significance
* avoid introducing major unrelated evidence
* indicate possible future research

---

# 🔮 105. FUTURE RESEARCH

A research project may identify areas that remain unexplored.

Example:

> A study of postcolonial novels may suggest future research on digital postcolonial literature.

# 🚨 TOP NET CONFUSIONS

## 1. Primary vs Secondary

**Primary = object/original evidence**

**Secondary = analysis of it**

---

## 2. Method vs Methodology

**Method = technique**

**Methodology = rationale/systematic approach**

---

## 3. Validity vs Reliability

**Validity = accuracy**

**Reliability = consistency**

---

## 4. Fabrication vs Falsification

**Fabrication = invent**

**Falsification = manipulate**

---

## 5. Correlation vs Causation

**Correlation ≠ causation**

---

## 6. Qualitative vs Quantitative

**Qualitative = meaning/words**

**Quantitative = numbers/measurement**

---

## 7. MLA vs APA

**MLA → Literature/Humanities → Works Cited → Author-page**

**APA → Social Sciences → References → Author-date**

---

## 8. Footnote vs Endnote

**Footnote → bottom of page**

**Endnote → end of chapter/document**

---

## 9. Abstract vs Introduction

**Abstract → compressed summary of entire research**

**Introduction → establishes topic/problem/context and research direction**

---

## 10. Bibliography vs Works Cited

**Works Cited → works actually cited**

**Bibliography → potentially broader list of consulted/relevant sources**
