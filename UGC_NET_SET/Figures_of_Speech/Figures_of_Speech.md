# 📚 UGC NET English: Figures of Speech

Detailed Notes with Definitions, Examples, and Exam Tricks

Figures of speech are words or expressions used in a non-literal, imaginative, or rhetorically effective way. They help writers create imagery, emotion, humour, emphasis, and multiple layers of meaning.

For UGC NET, focus on three things: definition, example, and the difference between confusing terms.

## 1. 🌹 Metaphor

Definition: A metaphor makes an implied comparison between two unlike things by treating one as the other, without using “like” or “as.”

📖 Examples:

* “Time is a thief.” Time is compared to a thief because it takes away moments and opportunities.

* “Juliet is the sun.” In Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, Juliet is associated with the sun to express her brightness and importance.

🧠 Exam tip: Metaphor = implied comparison.

## 2. 🌼 Simile

Definition: A simile explicitly compares two unlike things, usually using “like” or “as.”

📖 Examples:

* “Her smile was like sunshine.”

* “My love is like a red, red rose.” From Robert Burns's poem “A Red, Red Rose.”

🧠 Difference: A metaphor says “Her smile was sunshine”; a simile says “Her smile was like sunshine.”

## 3. 🌳 Personification

Definition: Personification gives human qualities, emotions, or actions to non-human things, animals, or abstract ideas.

📖 Examples:

* “The wind whispered through the trees.”

* “Death lays his icy hand on kings.” From James Shirley's poem “The Glories of Our Blood and State.”

Here, the wind is given the human action of whispering, and Death is represented as having a hand.

🧠 Exam tip: Personification is a type of figurative representation, but not every metaphor is personification.

## 4. 🎭 Metonymy

Definition: Metonymy replaces the name of something with another word closely associated with it.

📖 Examples:

* “The Crown announced a new policy.” The Crown represents the monarchy or royal authority.

* “The pen is mightier than the sword.” The pen represents writing or ideas; the sword represents military force.

🧠 Exam trick: Metonymy = association or connection.

## 5. 🧩 Synecdoche

Definition: Synecdoche is a figure of speech in which a part represents the whole, or the whole represents a part.

📖 Examples:

* “All hands on deck.” Hands means sailors or crew members.

* “Nice wheels!” Wheels means a car.

🧠 Difference: Synecdoche is based on a part-whole relationship; metonymy is based on a broader association.

For example, “the Crown” for the monarchy is metonymy, while “hands” for workers is synecdoche.

## 6. 📢 Hyperbole

Definition: Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration used for emphasis, humour, or emotional effect.

📖 Examples:

* “I've told you a million times.”

* “I'm so hungry I could eat a horse.”

The speaker does not literally mean a million repetitions or an entire horse.

🧠 Exam tip: Hyperbole is intentional exaggeration, not an accidental factual mistake.

## 7. 🤏 Litotes

Definition: Litotes expresses an idea by denying its opposite, often creating understatement.

📖 Examples:

* “She is not unattractive.” This implies that she is attractive.

* “That was no small achievement.” This implies that it was a significant achievement.

Litotes can communicate praise, criticism, or emphasis indirectly.

🧠 Exam trick: Look for a negative expression that implies a positive or stronger statement.

## 8. ⚡ Oxymoron

Definition: An oxymoron combines two apparently contradictory words or terms.

📖 Examples:

* “Deafening silence”

* “Bittersweet”

* “Sweet sorrow,” used by Shakespeare in Romeo and Juliet.

The contradiction creates a compact, striking effect.

🧠 Exam tip: Oxymoron usually operates within a short phrase.

## 9. 🧠 Paradox

Definition: A paradox is a statement or idea that appears contradictory or impossible but may reveal a deeper truth.

📖 Examples:

* “Less is more.”

* “I must be cruel only to be kind.” From Shakespeare's Hamlet.

Hamlet's statement presents cruelty as a means of achieving a supposedly beneficial outcome.

🧠 Difference: An oxymoron combines contradictory terms; a paradox presents an apparently contradictory idea or statement.

## 10. 😂 Pun

Definition: A pun is a play on words that exploits multiple meanings or similar sounds to produce humour or another rhetorical effect.

📖 Example:

In Shakespeare's Romeo and Juliet, Mercutio says, “Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a grave man.”

The word “grave” means both serious and associated with a grave or death.

Another invented example: “The bicycle couldn't stand on its own because it was two-tired.”

🧠 Exam tip: Pun = double meaning or sound-based wordplay.

## 11. 🎭 Irony

Definition: Irony involves a contrast between what is expected and what happens, what is said and what is meant, or what appears true and what is actually true.

Major types:

* Verbal irony: A speaker's intended meaning differs from the literal words.

* Situational irony: An outcome contradicts reasonable expectations.

* Dramatic irony: The audience knows something that a character does not.

📖 Examples:

* Verbal: Saying “Lovely weather!” during a terrible storm.

* Situational: A fire station catches fire.

* Dramatic: In Oedipus Rex, the audience may know the truth about Oedipus's identity before he recognises it.

🧠 Exam tip: Dramatic irony is particularly important in tragedy.

## 12. 😏 Sarcasm

Definition: Sarcasm is a sharp, mocking, or cutting form of expression, often used to ridicule or criticise.

📖 Example:

Someone arrives an hour late, and a friend says, “Wonderful punctuality!”

The words sound positive, but the intention is to criticise the person's lateness.

🧠 Difference: Sarcasm is often hostile or mocking; verbal irony can be gentle, neutral, playful, or critical. Sarcasm is not the same as every kind of irony.

## 13. 📖 Allusion

Definition: An allusion is an indirect reference to a well-known person, literary work, historical event, myth, or cultural idea.

📖 Examples:

* Calling someone “a Romeo” alludes to Shakespeare's romantic hero.

* Calling a difficult task “an Herculean labour” alludes to Hercules and his legendary strength.

Writers use allusions to create extra meaning without explaining the reference in full.

🧠 Exam tip: Allusion is a reference, not necessarily a direct quotation.

## 14. 🗣️ Apostrophe

Definition: Apostrophe is a figure of speech in which a speaker directly addresses an absent person, a dead person, an abstract concept, or a non-human entity.

📖 Examples:

* “O Death, where is thy sting?” from the biblical tradition, echoed in literary writing.

* “O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn's being.” From P. B. Shelley's “Ode to the West Wind.”

The speaker directly addresses the wind as though it could receive the words.

🧠 Exam trick: Apostrophe in rhetoric is not the punctuation mark. Look for direct address to someone or something absent or non-human.

## 15. 🔗 Zeugma

Definition: Zeugma occurs when one word, often a verb, governs or relates to two or more other words in different senses or in a strikingly unequal way.

📖 Example:

“She broke his car and his heart.”

The verb “broke” applies both to a physical object and to an emotional relationship.

Another famous example, from Alexander Pope's The Rape of the Lock:

“Here thou, great Anna! whom three realms obey,

Dost sometimes counsel take—and sometimes tea.”

The verb “take” applies to both counsel and tea, producing a witty contrast.

🧠 Exam tip: Zeugma often creates wit by making one word do double duty.

## 16. 🔄 Chiasmus

Definition: Chiasmus is a rhetorical arrangement in which words, grammatical structures, or ideas are repeated in reverse order.

📖 Famous example:

“Ask not what your country can do for you; ask what you can do for your country.”

This famous formulation by John F. Kennedy reverses the relationship between “country” and “you.”

A simpler invented example:

“She has all my love; my love belongs to her.”

🧠 Exam tip: Chiasmus = reversed structure, often represented as ABBA.

A related term is antimetabole, which specifically repeats the same words in reverse order. Some rhetorical traditions use chiasmus more broadly, so the two terms can overlap.
