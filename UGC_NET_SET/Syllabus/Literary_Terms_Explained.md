# UGC NET English: Important Literary Concepts & Terms — Detailed Notes

# 1. Narratology: The Study of Narrative

Narratology is the study of how stories are structured, narrated, and understood. It examines who tells the story, whose perspective we receive, how events are arranged, and how the reader interprets them.

## A. Unreliable Narrator

Definition: An unreliable narrator is a narrator whose account of events cannot be fully trusted by the reader.

The narrator may lie, misunderstand events, hide facts, be biased, or lack the maturity to understand what is happening.



Example 1: The Tell-Tale Heart — Edgar Allan Poe

The narrator repeatedly insists that he is sane while describing the careful planning and violent murder of an old man. His obsessive reasoning and disturbing perceptions make the reader question his version of reality.

Why unreliable? His own words undermine his claim of sanity.



Example 2: The Great Gatsby — F. Scott Fitzgerald

Nick Carraway narrates Gatsby's story. Nick claims to reserve judgment, yet his admiration for Gatsby influences how he presents characters and events.

Exam nuance: Nick is often discussed as a potentially biased or partially unreliable narrator, although his reliability is more complex than that of Poe's narrator.



Example 3: Lolita — Vladimir Nabokov

Humbert Humbert narrates his abuse of Dolores Haze in persuasive, elegant language. He attempts to manipulate the reader into accepting his self-justifications.

Why unreliable? His rhetoric conceals the moral reality of his actions.

Common types of unreliable narrators

* Lying narrator: Deliberately conceals or distorts facts.

* Naïve narrator: Misunderstands events because of limited experience.

* Biased narrator: Interprets events through personal prejudices.

* Impaired narrator: Has perceptions affected by psychological disturbance, intoxication, or memory problems.

UGC NET key point: An unreliable narrator is not necessarily a first-person narrator, and an unreliable account does not automatically mean every statement is false.

## B. First-Person Narrator

A first-person narrator uses pronouns such as I, me, and we to narrate events.

Example: Jane Eyre by Charlotte Brontë, in which Jane tells her own story.

Remember: First-person narration and unreliable narration are not synonyms. A first-person narrator can be highly reliable, unreliable, or somewhere in between.

## C. Third-Person Narrator

A third-person narrator refers to characters as he, she, they, or by their names.

There are two important possibilities:

* Third-person omniscient: The narrator can reveal the thoughts, feelings, and experiences of multiple characters.

* Third-person limited: The narration largely follows the knowledge or perspective of one character.

Example: In many passages of Harry Potter, narration closely follows Harry's experiences even though the story is told in the third person.

## D. Omniscient Narrator

An omniscient narrator is an all-knowing narrator who can provide information beyond the knowledge of any single character.

Example: George Eliot's Middlemarch contains a narrator capable of commenting on multiple characters and their social world.

Exam distinction: Omniscience concerns the narrator's knowledge; reliability concerns whether the narration deserves trust.

## E. Focalization

Focalization concerns whose perspective filters the information presented in a narrative.

The term is strongly associated with Gérard Genette.

Zero focalization

The narrator knows more than the character.

Example: A narrator reveals the secret thoughts of several characters who do not know one another's secrets.

Internal focalization

Information is restricted to what a particular character knows, perceives, or experiences.

Example: A scene presents only Elizabeth Bennet's understanding of Darcy's behaviour.

External focalization

The narrator reports observable behaviour without revealing the characters' private thoughts.

Example: A narrator describes a man entering a room, sitting down, and opening a letter, but does not tell us what he thinks.

Memory trick: Zero = narrator knows more; internal = narrator knows what the character knows; external = narrator reveals less than the character may know.

## F. Stream of Consciousness

Stream of consciousness is a narrative technique that attempts to represent the continuous, shifting flow of a character's thoughts, sensations, memories, and associations.

It can move rapidly between the present, the past, fantasy, and fleeting impressions.



Example: Mrs Dalloway — Virginia Woolf

Clarissa's present experiences trigger memories, emotions, and reflections. External events mingle with her inner life.



Example: Ulysses — James Joyce

Joyce uses experimental narration, including interior thought and the famous extended final monologue of Molly Bloom.

An invented example:

The rain again, that station, Mother's blue umbrella, the train is late, always late, and why did I say those words yesterday?

This imitates the mind's movement through sensation, memory, and association.

Key point: Stream of consciousness is a broad literary technique, not simply a synonym for a character thinking.

## G. Interior Monologue

Interior monologue presents a character's inner thoughts directly, as though the reader has access to the character's mind.

Example:

I should call her. No, not today. What would I even say?

This is an invented example of interior monologue.

Difference to remember:

* Stream of consciousness aims to represent the flow of consciousness.

* Interior monologue is a technique for presenting inner thought and may be one way of creating stream-of-consciousness narration.

## H. Free Indirect Discourse

Free indirect discourse blends a character's thoughts or language with third-person narration, usually without quotation marks or an explicit phrase such as “she thought.”

Example:

Elizabeth looked at Darcy. How unbearably proud he was!

The exclamation reflects Elizabeth's viewpoint, although the sentence remains in third-person narration.

Important association: Jane Austen is a major author to study for free indirect discourse.

Compare:

| Technique               | Example                        |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Direct thought          | She thought, “He is proud.”    |
| Indirect thought        | She thought that he was proud. |
| Free indirect discourse | He was proud indeed!           |

In the last sentence, the narrator's voice and the character's perspective overlap.

## I. Frame Narrative

A frame narrative is a story that contains one or more other stories within it.

Example: Frankenstein by Mary Shelley uses a layered narrative structure: Robert Walton's letters frame Victor Frankenstein's account, which includes the Creature's own narrative.

Exam clue: A story inside another story = frame narrative or embedded narrative.

## J. Analepsis and Prolepsis

These terms are associated with Gérard Genette's narratology.

* Analepsis: A flashback; the narrative returns to an earlier event.

* Prolepsis: A flashforward; the narrative anticipates a later event.

Examples:

* Analepsis: A character remembers their childhood while sitting in the present.

* Prolepsis: The narrator tells us that the character will regret this decision years later.

Memory trick: Analepsis = backward; prolepsis = forward.

## K. Fabula and Syuzhet

These concepts originated in Russian Formalist discussions of narrative.

* Fabula: The events of a story in their chronological or logical order.

* Syuzhet: The order and manner in which those events are presented in the text.

Example: A novel begins with a murder, reveals the victim's identity later, and finally shows the events leading to the crime.

The chronological chain of events is the fabula; the deliberately arranged presentation is the syuzhet.

## L. Metalepsis

Metalepsis occurs when the boundary between different narrative levels is crossed.

Example: A fictional character suddenly addresses the author who created them, or a narrator enters the story they are narrating.

Exam distinction: A story within a story is a frame narrative; a crossing between narrative levels is metalepsis.

# 2. Modernism and Postmodernism

These literary movements are important for UGC NET because many narrative techniques and theoretical concepts are associated with them.

A. Modernism

Modernism responds to the uncertainties of the modern world through experimentation, fragmentation, subjective experience, and challenges to traditional literary forms.

Major writers: James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, and William Faulkner.

Example: The Waste Land by T. S. Eliot uses fragmented voices, literary allusions, and multiple cultural references.

B. Postmodernism

Postmodernism frequently questions fixed truths, stable identities, grand explanations of history, and the distinction between reality and representation.

Major writers: John Barth, Thomas Pynchon, Kurt Vonnegut, and Italo Calvino.

Example: If on a winter's night a traveler by Italo Calvino draws attention to the act of reading and the construction of fiction.

## M. Metafiction

Metafiction is fiction that draws attention to its own fictional status or construction.

Example: A narrator says, “I could make my hero marry her, but that would be too easy, so I shall begin again.”

The text openly reminds us that the author is constructing a story.

Key association: Patricia Waugh's Metafiction: The Theory and Practice of Self-Conscious Fiction is a major critical work on the concept.

## N. Historiographic Metafiction

A term associated with Linda Hutcheon.

It describes postmodern historical fiction that both revisits the past and questions how history is written and represented.

Example: The French Lieutenant's Woman by John Fowles draws attention to the conventions of the historical novel and the author's role in constructing the narrative.

Distinction: Metafiction questions the construction of fiction; historiographic metafiction also engages critically with the construction of historical knowledge.

## O. Intertextuality

Intertextuality refers to the relationships between texts. A literary work can echo, quote, transform, or respond to earlier works.

The concept is strongly associated with Julia Kristeva, who developed it from Mikhail Bakhtin's ideas about dialogism.

Examples:

* Ulysses by James Joyce reworks elements of Homer's Odyssey.

* Wide Sargasso Sea by Jean Rhys reimagines the world of Charlotte Brontë's Jane Eyre from the perspective of Bertha Mason, here given the name Antoinette Cosway.

UGC NET clue: Julia Kristeva = intertextuality.

## P. Pastiche

Pastiche imitates or combines the styles of existing authors, genres, or artistic traditions. It may be appreciative, playful, or critical.

Example: A novel deliberately written in the style of a Victorian detective story.

Pastiche is particularly associated with discussions of postmodernism and is discussed by Fredric Jameson in his account of postmodern culture.

## Q. Parody

Parody imitates a work, author, or genre for comic or critical effect.

Example: Don Quixote by Miguel de Cervantes parodies the conventions of chivalric romance.

Difference:

* Parody often mocks or critiques its model.

* Pastiche imitates a style and need not ridicule it.

## R. Defamiliarization

Defamiliarization makes familiar things appear strange or new so that readers perceive them differently.

The concept is associated with Russian Formalist Viktor Shklovsky, especially his essay Art as Technique (also translated as Art as Device).

Example: Instead of writing “the sun rose,” a writer describes the morning light as a burning curtain slowly opening over the world.

The unusual description forces the reader to notice an everyday experience anew.

Exam keyword: Russian Formalism → Viktor Shklovsky → Defamiliarization.

## S. Magic Realism

Magic realism combines realistic settings and ordinary social life with supernatural or extraordinary events presented as though they are normal.

Example: One Hundred Years of Solitude by Gabriel García Márquez.

The supernatural is not necessarily treated as a disruption of reality; it becomes part of the characters' ordinary world.

Do not confuse it with fantasy: Magic realism generally retains a recognisably realistic social and historical world.

# 3. Literary Criticism: The Most Important Concepts

These concepts frequently appear in questions on literary theory and critical terminology.

## T. Intentional Fallacy

Associated with W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe C. Beardsley.

The intentional fallacy is the mistake of treating an author's intention as the final authority for determining a literary work's meaning.

Example: A poet writes about a storm. Even if the poet says the storm represents happiness, a critic can still examine the poem's actual language, structure, and imagery to argue that it conveys fear.

Key point: The author's stated intention does not automatically determine the text's meaning.

## U. Affective Fallacy

Also associated with Wimsatt and Beardsley.

The affective fallacy is the mistake of judging or interpreting a literary work primarily through the emotional effects it produces in readers.

Example: Saying a poem is excellent solely because it made you cry does not constitute a sufficient analysis of its literary qualities.

Difference:

* Intentional fallacy → excessive reliance on authorial intention.

* Affective fallacy → excessive reliance on readers' emotional responses.

Both are important to New Criticism.

## V. Objective Correlative

Associated with T. S. Eliot, particularly his essay Hamlet and His Problems.

An objective correlative is a set of objects, situations, or events that effectively evokes a particular emotion.

Example: A character's grief is conveyed through an empty chair, an untouched cup of tea, and a room that has not been changed since a loved one's death.

Rather than naming the emotion directly, the writer provides concrete details through which the reader experiences it.

Exam clue: T. S. Eliot = objective correlative.

## W. Intentionality Versus Intentional Fallacy

Do not confuse these terms.

* Intentionality in philosophy generally concerns the directedness of consciousness toward objects, ideas, or states of affairs.

* Intentional fallacy in literary criticism concerns the mistake of using the author's intention as the ultimate standard for interpreting a work.

## X. The Death of the Author

Associated with Roland Barthes, particularly his 1967 essay “The Death of the Author,” published in English in 1967.

The argument challenges the idea that an author's biography or intention should have the final say in interpreting a text.

Example: A reader analyses a poem about power by examining its language, contradictions, and cultural meanings rather than relying only on what its writer says the poem means.

Exam distinction: Wimsatt and Beardsley are associated with the intentional fallacy; Barthes is associated with “The Death of the Author.”

## Y. Ambiguity

Ambiguity occurs when a word, phrase, or passage allows more than one plausible interpretation.

Example: “She saw the man with the telescope.”

Did she use the telescope to see the man, or did the man have the telescope?

In literary writing, ambiguity can enrich a text by sustaining multiple meanings.

Important critic: William Empson, author of Seven Types of Ambiguity.

## Z. Paradox

A paradox is a statement that appears contradictory or absurd but may reveal a deeper truth.

Example: “Less is more.”

In literature, paradox can expose contradictions in human experience, language, or society.

Remember: Ambiguity involves multiple possible meanings; paradox involves an apparent contradiction.

# 4. Structuralism and Poststructuralism

These are essential for literary theory questions in UGC NET English.

## AA. Signifier and Signified

Associated with the linguist Ferdinand de Saussure.

* Signifier: The form of a sign, such as a word, sound, or written symbol.

* Signified: The concept associated with that sign.

Example: The written word “tree” is the signifier; the concept of a tree in the mind is the signified.

Together, they form a linguistic sign.

Saussure argued that the relationship between the signifier and signified is generally arbitrary: there is no natural reason why English speakers use the word tree rather than another sound sequence.

## AB. Structuralism

Structuralism studies the underlying systems, rules, and relationships that produce meaning.

Rather than treating a literary work as entirely independent, structuralists examine the conventions and structures that make it intelligible.

Major thinkers:

* Ferdinand de Saussure — structural linguistics.

* Claude Lévi-Strauss — structural anthropology and myth.

* Roland Barthes — structuralist literary and cultural analysis.

* Gérard Genette — structural narratology.

Example: A structuralist analysis of a fairy tale might examine recurring oppositions such as nature/culture, innocence/experience, or order/chaos.

## AC. Binary Opposition

Binary opposition refers to pairs of contrasting concepts used to organise meaning.

Examples:

* Nature / Culture

* Male / Female

* Speech / Writing

* Civilised / Primitive

* Good / Evil

Structuralists study how such oppositions organise cultural narratives. Poststructuralists question whether one side can be given a stable or superior meaning.

## AD. Poststructuralism

Poststructuralism challenges the idea that linguistic structures provide a completely stable foundation for meaning.

It emphasises instability, contradiction, differences between interpretations, and the limits of fixed systems.

Major thinkers: Jacques Derrida, Roland Barthes, Michel Foucault, and Julia Kristeva.

## AE. Deconstruction

Associated with Jacques Derrida.

Deconstruction examines tensions, contradictions, and assumptions within a text, especially where the text undermines its own apparent argument or hierarchy.

Example: A text may praise speech as authentic while relying on writing to preserve and communicate its argument. A deconstructive reading examines the instability of that hierarchy.

Important: Deconstruction does not simply mean destroying a text or saying that it has no meaning.

## AF. Différance

A term associated with Jacques Derrida.

Différance draws attention to two connected ideas:

1. Meaning emerges through differences between signs.

2. Meaning is continually deferred through relationships with other signs.

Example: To understand the word “day,” we distinguish it from words such as “night,” “evening,” and “darkness.” Its meaning also depends on the linguistic context in which it appears.

The spelling différance combines the senses of differing and deferring in Derrida's argument.

Exam association: Derrida → Deconstruction → Différance.

# 5. Psychoanalytic Literary Criticism

Psychoanalytic criticism explores unconscious desires, repression, dreams, anxieties, sexuality, and the formation of identity.

AG. Sigmund Freud

Key concepts: unconscious, repression, dreams, id, ego, superego, Oedipus complex.

Example: A character's disproportionate fear of abandonment might be interpreted in relation to repressed experiences or unconscious conflict.

AH. Carl Jung

Key concepts: collective unconscious, archetypes, persona, shadow, anima and animus.

Example: A recurring wise old guide in a story may be interpreted as an archetypal figure.

AI. Jacques Lacan

Key concepts: Mirror Stage, Imaginary, Symbolic, Real, desire, the unconscious structured like a language.

Example: A character's identity may be understood as developing through identification with images of the self and through language and social structures.

## AJ. The Oedipus Complex

A Freudian concept referring to a child's unconscious desires and rivalries within the family, especially as Freud theorised them in relation to the parents.

The term derives from the Greek myth of Oedipus, who unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother.

Literary application: A critic might use the concept to examine family rivalry, forbidden desire, guilt, or the symbolic significance of parental relationships.

## AK. Archetype

An archetype is a recurring symbolic pattern, character type, image, or narrative situation.

Examples:

* The hero

* The trickster

* The wise old person

* The journey

* Death and rebirth

Archetypal criticism is associated with Jung and, in literary criticism, with Northrop Frye, author of Anatomy of Criticism.

Distinction: Jung's archetypes are linked to the collective unconscious; Frye developed a systematic account of recurring literary conventions, myths, and narrative patterns.

## AL. The Mirror Stage

Associated with Jacques Lacan.

The Mirror Stage describes a formative process in which an infant identifies with an image of a unified body, helping to establish the ego while also involving a fundamental misrecognition.

Exam clue: Lacan → Mirror Stage → identity and misrecognition.

# 6. Feminist Literary Theory

Feminist criticism examines gender, patriarchy, representation, power, women's writing, and the social construction of gender roles.

## AM. Gynocriticism

Associated with Elaine Showalter.

Gynocriticism studies women as writers, examining women's literary traditions, authorship, themes, genres, and historical conditions.

Showalter discusses this approach in “Towards a Feminist Poetics” and A Literature of Their Own.

Example: Studying how women novelists represent female experience and how women's literary traditions developed under patriarchal publishing systems.

Important distinction:

* Feminist critique may examine the representation of women in texts written by men.

* Gynocriticism focuses on women as writers and on women's literary production.

## AN. Androcentrism

Androcentrism means treating male experience, perspectives, or interests as the universal norm.

Example: A literary history that presents male authors as the default representatives of human experience while treating women writers as a separate, minor category may be criticised as androcentric.

## AO. The Male Gaze

A concept strongly associated with Laura Mulvey, especially her 1975 essay “Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema.”

The male gaze describes ways in which visual representation positions women as objects of masculine viewing and desire.

Although developed in film theory, the concept can also inform the analysis of novels, advertisements, and other cultural forms.

## AP. Performativity of Gender

Associated with Judith Butler, particularly Gender Trouble (1990).

Gender performativity refers to how gender is constituted and sustained through repeated acts, gestures, behaviours, and social norms.

It does not simply mean that a person consciously chooses to perform a role whenever they wish.

Example: Repeated social expectations about clothing, speech, occupations, and behaviour can help produce the appearance that gender roles are natural and fixed.

## AQ. Écriture Féminine

Associated especially with Hélène Cixous.

Écriture féminine refers to a mode of writing that challenges dominant, phallocentric traditions of language and representation.

It is linked to Cixous's essay “The Laugh of the Medusa.”

Exam association: Hélène Cixous → Écriture féminine.

# 7. Marxism, Ideology, and Cultural Studies

## AR. Ideology

Ideology refers to systems of ideas, values, and beliefs through which people understand society. In Marxist criticism, ideology is often examined in relation to class relations and the maintenance of power.

Example: A novel that presents inherited wealth as proof of moral superiority may be analysed for the ideological assumptions it reproduces.

## AS. Base and Superstructure

Associated with Marxist social theory.

* Base: The economic organisation of society, including relations and conditions of production.

* Superstructure: Political, legal, cultural, and ideological institutions and practices associated with that social organisation.

Literature is often studied in relation to the economic and social conditions in which it is produced, circulated, and read.

Important nuance: Marxist criticism includes different explanations of the relationship between economics and culture; it should not always be reduced to the claim that economics mechanically determines every literary detail.

## AT. Hegemony

Associated especially with Antonio Gramsci.

Hegemony describes how a dominant group maintains leadership and power partly by securing consent and making its values appear natural or common sense.

Example: Workers may accept an unequal social arrangement because dominant cultural beliefs portray it as inevitable or fair.

Exam clue: Gramsci → Hegemony.

## AU. Reification

In Marxist theory, reification describes the treatment of social relations between people as if they were natural relationships between things.

Example: A literary work may depict workers as replaceable commodities, making human labour appear equivalent to an object with a market price.

## AV. Cultural Materialism

Cultural Materialism examines literature in relation to historical conditions, institutions, social power, and ideology.

It is especially associated with British literary criticism and figures such as Raymond Williams and Jonathan Dollimore.

Example: Studying Shakespeare's plays in relation to the political and ideological structures of Elizabethan and Jacobean England.

## AW. New Historicism

Associated especially with Stephen Greenblatt.

New Historicism studies literary works in relation to the discourses, institutions, and power relations of their historical moment. It often examines literary and non-literary texts together.

Example: Analysing The Tempest alongside early modern travel writing, colonial discourse, and contemporary ideas about authority.

Difference from a simple historical reading: New Historicism emphasises the interaction of literature, culture, discourse, and power rather than merely using historical facts to explain a text.

# 8. Postcolonial Literary Theory

Postcolonial criticism examines colonialism, imperialism, race, identity, language, cultural representation, resistance, and the continuing effects of colonial power.

## AX. Orientalism

Associated with Edward Said, especially his book Orientalism (1978).

Orientalism examines how Western scholarship, literature, and cultural representations constructed “the Orient” as exotic, irrational, backward, or inferior in contrast to a supposedly rational and superior West.

Example: A colonial novel that depicts an Eastern society as mysterious and incapable of governing itself can be analysed through Said's concept of Orientalism.

Exam association: Edward Said → Orientalism.

## AY. Subaltern

Associated especially with Antonio Gramsci and later Subaltern Studies, including the work of Ranajit Guha and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak.

The term refers to groups marginalised from dominant structures of political, economic, and cultural power.

Spivak's essay “Can the Subaltern Speak?” examines the difficulties of representing marginalised people within dominant systems of knowledge.

Important: Spivak's argument is not simply that oppressed people are literally incapable of speaking. It concerns whether their voices can be heard, represented, and recognised within dominant structures.

## AZ. Hybridity

Associated particularly with Homi K. Bhabha.

Hybridity refers to cultural forms and identities produced through contact, negotiation, and unequal relations between cultures.

Example: A postcolonial writer combines English literary conventions with Indian cultural references and linguistic patterns to produce a form that belongs neatly to neither a purely British nor a purely traditional Indian category.

## BA. Mimicry

Also associated with Homi K. Bhabha.

Colonial mimicry involves the colonised subject's imitation of the coloniser's language, manners, and institutions. Such imitation can reinforce colonial authority while also exposing its contradictions.

Memory trick: Hybridity = cultural negotiation and mixture; mimicry = imitation that can be ambivalent and unsettling.

## BB. Double Consciousness

Associated with W. E. B. Du Bois, particularly The Souls of Black Folk (1903).

Double consciousness describes the experience of seeing oneself through one's own perspective and through the perspective of a society that devalues or racialises one's identity.

Example: A character struggles between a sense of personal identity and the racial stereotypes imposed by the surrounding society.

Although influential in postcolonial and identity studies, the concept originated in Du Bois's analysis of Black experience in the United States.

## BC. Diaspora

Diaspora refers to the dispersal of a population away from an ancestral homeland, often involving questions of memory, belonging, migration, cultural identity, and displacement.

Examples of writers useful for study include Salman Rushdie, Jhumpa Lahiri, and V. S. Naipaul.

Exam distinction: Diaspora concerns dispersion and belonging; hybridity concerns the formation and negotiation of cultural identities.

# 9. Reader-Response Theory

Reader-response criticism investigates the reader's role in producing or interpreting literary meaning.

## BD. The Implied Reader

The implied reader is the hypothetical reader presupposed by a text's structure, knowledge, conventions, and demands.

The concept is associated with Wolfgang Iser, among others.

Example: A novel full of allusions to Greek mythology presupposes a reader who can recognise, or learn to recognise, those references.

## BE. Interpretive Communities

Associated with Stanley Fish.

Interpretive communities are groups whose shared assumptions, practices, and reading strategies shape how they interpret texts.

Example: A feminist critic and a formalist critic may interpret the same novel differently because they approach it with different critical frameworks.

## BF. The Intentional Reader and the Actual Reader

It is useful to distinguish several concepts:

* Actual reader: A real person who reads the text.

* Implied reader: The reader-position anticipated by the text.

* Interpretive community: A group whose shared interpretive practices influence meaning.

Do not treat these terms as interchangeable.

## BG. The Horizon of Expectations

Associated with Hans Robert Jauss and reception aesthetics.

A horizon of expectations is the set of literary conventions, cultural assumptions, and reading expectations that readers bring to a work at a particular historical moment.

Example: A novel that initially shocks readers may later be regarded as conventional because literary expectations have changed.

Exam association: Jauss → Reception aesthetics → Horizon of expectations.

# 10. Other Highly Testable Literary Concepts

## BH. Catharsis

Associated with Aristotle's Poetics.

Catharsis is connected with tragedy's arousal and resolution or purgation of pity and fear, although its precise meaning has been debated.

Example: The tragic suffering in Oedipus Rex may lead the audience to experience pity and fear.

Exam clue: Aristotle → Tragedy → Catharsis.

## BI. Hamartia

Hamartia is the error, mistake, or failing that contributes to a tragic character's downfall in Aristotle's account of tragedy.

Example: Oedipus's determination to discover the truth leads him towards the terrible recognition of his own identity and actions.

Important nuance: Hamartia should not automatically be translated as a simple moral flaw. Its interpretation is debated.

## BJ. Peripeteia

Peripeteia means a reversal of circumstances or fortune in a dramatic plot.

Example: A character who believes they have escaped danger discovers that their actions have brought about the very outcome they feared.

## BK. Anagnorisis

Anagnorisis is a moment of recognition or discovery, particularly a transition from ignorance to knowledge in tragedy.

In Oedipus Rex, Oedipus discovers the truth about his parentage and his relationship to the crimes he has been investigating.

Quick comparison:

| Term        | Meaning                                  |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Hamartia    | Error or failing                         |
| Peripeteia  | Reversal                                 |
| Anagnorisis | Recognition                              |
| Catharsis   | Pity and fear and their cathartic effect |

## BL. Pathetic Fallacy

A term introduced by John Ruskin.

Pathetic fallacy occurs when human feelings are attributed to nature or inanimate things.

Example: “The angry clouds gathered above the grieving town.”

The clouds are described as though they experience human emotions.

## BM. Sublime

The sublime refers to an experience of overwhelming grandeur, terror, vastness, or power that exceeds ordinary comprehension.

Important thinkers include:

* Longinus — On the Sublime.

* Edmund Burke — A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful.

* Immanuel Kant — philosophical accounts of the sublime.

Example: A character confronting a vast mountain range may feel both fear and awe at a scale that exceeds human power.

## BN. Ekphrasis

Ekphrasis is a verbal representation of a visual work of art.

Example: John Keats's “Ode on a Grecian Urn” describes and reflects on the figures and scenes represented on an imagined ancient urn.

Exam association: Keats → “Ode on a Grecian Urn” → Ekphrasis.

## BO. Objective Versus Subjective Narration

* Objective narration: Emphasises observable events, actions, and dialogue without extensive access to private thoughts.

* Subjective narration: Strongly reflects an individual's thoughts, perceptions, emotions, or biases.

These are useful descriptive terms, but they do not constitute a simple binary that fits every narrative.

## BP. Interpellation

Associated with Louis Althusser.

Interpellation describes how ideology “hails” individuals and positions them as subjects within a social order.

Example: A person responds to social expectations of being a “good citizen,” recognising themselves within an identity shaped by institutions and ideology.

## BQ. Panopticism

Associated with Michel Foucault, drawing on Jeremy Bentham's design for the Panopticon prison.

Panopticism describes a model of disciplinary power in which the possibility of constant observation encourages people to regulate their own behaviour.

Example: Students may follow institutional rules because they believe their conduct could be monitored at any time.

## BR. Dialogism and Heteroglossia

Associated with Mikhail Bakhtin.

* Dialogism: Meaning develops through interaction among voices, perspectives, and utterances.

* Heteroglossia: The coexistence of different social languages, registers, accents, and ideological perspectives within language and literature.

Example: A novel includes the speech of politicians, workers, religious authorities, and young people, each reflecting different social values and worldviews.

Exam association: Bakhtin → Dialogism, heteroglossia, polyphony, and the carnivalesque.

## BS. Polyphony

Bakhtin uses polyphony to describe a novel in which multiple voices or consciousnesses retain a degree of independence rather than being reduced to a single authoritative viewpoint.

Example: Bakhtin's study of Dostoevsky emphasises the plurality of independent voices in his novels.

## BT. Carnivalesque

The carnivalesque describes the temporary overturning or disruption of established hierarchies, conventions, and authority, often through humour, parody, bodily excess, and popular festivity.

Associated with Bakhtin's work on Rabelais and carnival culture.

# 11. Ecocriticism and Contemporary Approaches

## BU. Ecocriticism

Ecocriticism studies the relationship between literature, culture, and the physical environment.

Example: A reading of Wordsworth's poetry might examine how nature shapes human identity and challenges purely human-centred perspectives.

Important names include Cheryll Glotfelty, Lawrence Buell, and Greg Garrard.

## BV. Anthropocentrism

Anthropocentrism places human beings at the centre of moral, cultural, or intellectual concern.

Example: A novel may depict a forest solely as a resource for human profit, without acknowledging the value of the ecosystem itself.

## BW. Deep Ecology

Deep ecology, associated with Arne Næss, challenges the assumption that nature's value depends entirely on its usefulness to humans.

It emphasises the intrinsic value of non-human life and ecological systems.

## BX. Ecofeminism

Ecofeminism examines connections between the domination of women and the exploitation of nature, while recognising that ecofeminist approaches differ in their explanations and political commitments.

Example: A novel that links patriarchal authority, land ownership, and the destruction of a rural ecosystem could be examined through ecofeminist criticism.

## BY. Anthropocene

The Anthropocene is a proposed geological epoch or framework emphasising humanity's significant impact on Earth's systems. Its formal geological status has been debated.

In literary studies, the term is used to examine climate change, extinction, pollution, and the relationship between human activity and the planet.

# 14. How to Study These Concepts for UGC NET

I recommend studying them in this order:

1. Narratology: Narrator, unreliable narrator, focalization, stream of consciousness, free indirect discourse, analepsis and prolepsis.

2. Literary criticism: Intentional fallacy, affective fallacy, objective correlative, ambiguity and defamiliarization.

3. Literary theory: Structuralism, poststructuralism, deconstruction, intertextuality and reader-response theory.

4. Critical schools: Marxism, feminism, psychoanalysis, postcolonialism, New Historicism and ecocriticism.

5. Modernism and postmodernism: Fragmentation, metafiction, pastiche, parody and historiographic metafiction.

6. Practice: Solve previous-year questions and revise the thinkers, works, definitions, and examples associated with each term.
