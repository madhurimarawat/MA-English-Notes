# Literary Movements

The most useful way to study literary movements for UGC NET is to connect four things:

Movement → Period → Features → Writers → Works

## 1. Humanism

Period: Renaissance humanism, especially 14th–16th centuries

Definition: Humanism is an intellectual movement that emphasizes human dignity, reason, education, ethical responsibility, and the study of classical Greek and Roman literature.

Key features

* Studia humanitatis: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history, and moral philosophy.

* Revival of classical texts and languages.

* Emphasis on human agency and worldly experience.

* Philology, textual scholarship, and education.

* In Renaissance England, humanism contributed to the development of individual character and eloquent expression.

Major thinkers and writers

* Petrarch: Often called the father of Renaissance humanism.

* Erasmus: Christian humanist; advocated education and religious reform.

* Thomas More: Humanist thinker and author.

* Sir Thomas Elyot: Promoted humanist education.

* William Shakespeare: Explored human complexity and moral choice.

Important works

* Petrarch: Canzoniere

* Erasmus: In Praise of Folly (1511)

* Thomas More: Utopia (1516)

* Shakespeare: Hamlet, King Lear

UGC NET tip: Humanism is an intellectual orientation, not simply a literary genre. Renaissance humanism often sought to reconcile classical learning with Christian thought.

## 2. Renaissance

Period: Approximately 14th–17th centuries in Europe; English Renaissance, especially late 15th–early 17th centuries

Definition: The Renaissance, meaning “rebirth,” was a cultural revival of classical learning that transformed literature, art, science, and philosophy.

Key features

* Revival of classical Greek and Roman culture.

* Humanism and individualism.

* Exploration, scientific curiosity, and changing worldviews.

* Growth of drama, lyric poetry, sonnets, and prose.

* Increased influence of print culture.

* In England, flourishing drama during the Elizabethan and Jacobean periods.

Major writers and works

* William Shakespeare: Hamlet, Macbeth, The Tempest

* Christopher Marlowe: Doctor Faustus, Tamburlaine

* Edmund Spenser: The Faerie Queene

* Sir Philip Sidney: Astrophil and Stella, An Apology for Poetry

* Francis Bacon: Essays

* John Donne: Early metaphysical poetry

Important subperiods

* Elizabethan period: 1558–1603

* Jacobean period: 1603–1625

* Caroline period: 1625–1649

UGC NET tip: Renaissance is broader than Elizabethan literature. The Elizabethan age is one phase within the English Renaissance.

## 3. Reformation

Period: From 1517; English Reformation developed mainly during the 16th century

Definition: The Reformation was a religious movement that challenged aspects of the authority and practices of the Roman Catholic Church and led to the formation of Protestant traditions.

Key features

* Religious reform and disputes over church authority.

* Emphasis on scripture and individual faith in many Protestant traditions.

* Translation of the Bible into vernacular languages.

* Effects on religious poetry, sermons, polemics, and prose.

* Major changes in English church-state relations.

Major figures and writers

* Martin Luther: Initiated the Protestant Reformation in 1517.

* John Calvin: Major Protestant theologian.

* William Tyndale: English Bible translator.

* William Tyndale’s New Testament: Influential in the development of English biblical prose.

* John Foxe: Acts and Monuments, commonly known as Foxe’s Book of Martyrs.

* John Milton: Later Puritan writer whose work reflects Protestant religious and political concerns.

Important works

* Tyndale’s English New Testament (1526)

* Foxe’s Acts and Monuments (1563)

* Milton’s Paradise Lost (1667)

UGC NET tip: The English Reformation was closely connected to Henry VIII’s break with Rome, but the wider European Reformation began earlier with Luther.

## 4. Metaphysical poetry

Period: Mainly early 17th century

Definition: Metaphysical poetry is a form of 17th-century English poetry characterized by intellectual argument, philosophical reflection, startling comparisons, and the fusion of thought with emotion.

Key features

* Conceit: An elaborate, unexpected comparison.

* Wit and paradox.

* Argumentative or conversational openings.

* Exploration of love, religion, death, and the soul.

* Dramatic situations and colloquial diction.

* Fusion of intellectual reasoning and emotional intensity.

Major poets and works

* John Donne: “The Flea,” “A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning,” “The Canonization”

* George Herbert: The Temple, “The Collar”

* Andrew Marvell: “To His Coy Mistress,” “The Definition of Love”

* Henry Vaughan: Silex Scintillans

* Richard Crashaw: Steps to the Temple

Critical association: Samuel Johnson popularized the term “metaphysical poets” in Lives of the Most Eminent English Poets (1779–1781). T. S. Eliot later helped restore their critical reputation through “The Metaphysical Poets” (1921).

UGC NET tip: John Donne is the central figure. Remember the metaphysical conceit, such as the compass in “A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning.”

## 5. Neoclassicism

Period: Mainly late 17th to late 18th centuries in Britain

Definition: Neoclassicism is a literary movement that draws inspiration from classical Greek and Roman models and values order, decorum, balance, reason, and formal discipline.

Key features

* Imitation of classical writers such as Horace, Virgil, and Juvenal.

* Emphasis on reason, restraint, and artistic rules.

* Satire as a means of correcting social and moral faults.

* Heroic couplets and polished poetic diction.

* Decorum: style should suit subject, character, and genre.

* Preference for general truths and social observation.

Major writers and works

* John Dryden: Absalom and Achitophel, Mac Flecknoe

* Alexander Pope: The Rape of the Lock, An Essay on Criticism, An Essay on Man

* Jonathan Swift: Gulliver’s Travels, “A Modest Proposal”

* Samuel Johnson: The Vanity of Human Wishes, Preface to Shakespeare

* Joseph Addison and Richard Steele: The Spectator

Important terms

* Augustan Age: Early 18th-century literary culture associated with classical imitation and satire.

* Heroic couplet: Two rhyming lines, usually in iambic pentameter.

* Mock-epic: Uses epic conventions to treat a trivial subject grandly.

UGC NET tip: Pope’s The Rape of the Lock is the classic English mock-epic; Swift is central to satirical prose.

## 6. Restoration

Period: 1660–approximately 1700, beginning with the restoration of the monarchy

Definition: The Restoration refers to the return of Charles II to the English throne in 1660 and the literary culture associated with the period.

Key features

* Reopening of theatres after the Puritan closure.

* Popularity of Restoration comedy and comedy of manners.

* Wit, sexual intrigue, social satire, and urban sophistication.

* Heroic drama and political satire.

* Increased visibility of women on the professional English stage.

* Strong courtly and aristocratic influence.

Major writers and works

* John Dryden: All for Love, Absalom and Achitophel

* William Wycherley: The Country Wife

* William Congreve: The Way of the World

* George Etherege: The Man of Mode

* Aphra Behn: The Rover, Oroonoko

* John Bunyan: The Pilgrim’s Progress

UGC NET tip: Aphra Behn is especially important as one of the first English women to earn her living as a professional writer. Restoration comedy is frequently tested through its conventions and playwrights.

## 7. Romanticism

Period: Late 18th to mid-19th century; in Britain, often dated from 1798

Definition: Romanticism was a literary and artistic movement that emphasized imagination, emotion, individual experience, nature, freedom, and resistance to excessive rationalism.

Key features

* Imagination as a creative and potentially transformative power.

* Nature as a source of spiritual insight and emotional renewal.

* Interest in childhood, memory, dreams, and the supernatural.

* Celebration of the individual and rebellion against convention.

* Sympathy for ordinary people and revolutionary ideals.

* The sublime: experiences of awe, vastness, and sometimes terror.

Major writers and works

| Poet/writer        | Important works                                                |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| William Wordsworth | Lyrical Ballads (with Coleridge), “Tintern Abbey,” The Prelude |
| S. T. Coleridge    | “The Rime of the Ancient Mariner,” “Kubla Khan”                |
| William Blake      | Songs of Innocence and of Experience                           |
| Lord Byron         | Don Juan, Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage                           |
| P. B. Shelley      | “Ode to the West Wind,” Prometheus Unbound                     |
| John Keats         | “Ode to a Nightingale,” “Ode on a Grecian Urn”                 |
| Mary Shelley       | Frankenstein                                                   |
| Jane Austen        | Pride and Prejudice                                            |

Important critical concepts

* Wordsworth: poetry as the “spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings,” qualified by recollection and reflection.

* Coleridge: distinction between primary and secondary imagination; the “willing suspension of disbelief.”

* Keats: negative capability, the capacity to remain amid uncertainties without forcing a final explanation.

UGC NET tip: Lyrical Ballads (1798) is a conventional landmark of English Romanticism. Remember that Romanticism is not simply writing about nature; imagination, subjectivity, and resistance to restrictive conventions are equally important.

## 8. Victorianism

Period: 1837–1901, the reign of Queen Victoria

Definition: Victorian literature reflects the social, industrial, scientific, religious, and moral transformations of nineteenth-century Britain.

Key features

* Industrialization, urban poverty, and class conflict.

* Social criticism and reform.

* Tension between religious belief and scientific thought.

* Respectability, morality, gender roles, and domestic ideology.

* Growth of the realist novel and serialized fiction.

* Dramatic monologues and experiments with poetic form.

Major writers and works

* Charles Dickens: Oliver Twist, Bleak House, Great Expectations

* George Eliot: Middlemarch, The Mill on the Floss

* Charlotte Brontë: Jane Eyre

* Emily Brontë: Wuthering Heights

* Thomas Hardy: Tess of the d’Urbervilles, Jude the Obscure

* Alfred, Lord Tennyson: In Memoriam A.H.H., “Ulysses”

* Robert Browning: “My Last Duchess,” “Fra Lippo Lippi”

* Matthew Arnold: “Dover Beach,” Culture and Anarchy

* Christina Rossetti: “Goblin Market”

* Oscar Wilde: The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890/1891), at the Victorian period’s end

UGC NET tip: Victorianism is internally diverse. Dickens and Eliot are associated with social realism, Browning with the dramatic monologue, and Hardy with tragic naturalism and pessimistic determinism.

## 9. Pre-Raphaelite movement

Period: Founded in Britain in 1848; influence extended into later Victorian literature and art

Definition: The Pre-Raphaelite movement began as an artistic brotherhood that opposed what its members saw as the conventionality of later academic art, seeking greater detail, intensity, and inspiration from early Renaissance art and medieval subjects.

Key features

* Vivid imagery and minute descriptive detail.

* Medievalism, legend, myth, and Arthurian themes.

* Rich color, sensuous beauty, and symbolic detail.

* Nature depicted with close observation.

* Interest in idealized women, beauty, love, and mortality.

Major figures and works

* Dante Gabriel Rossetti: “The Blessed Damozel,” The House of Life

* Christina Rossetti: “Goblin Market,” “Remember”

* William Morris: The Defence of Guenevere

* Algernon Charles Swinburne: Poems and Ballads (1866), associated with the wider aesthetic and Pre-Raphaelite milieu.

Key names in the original Brotherhood: Dante Gabriel Rossetti, William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, and others.

UGC NET tip: Distinguish the original Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, founded in 1848, from the broader Pre-Raphaelite literary and artistic movement that developed afterward.

## 10. Aestheticism

Period: Especially 1860s–1890s

Definition: Aestheticism holds that art has value in its own right and need not justify itself through moral instruction or social utility.

Key features

* “Art for art’s sake.”

* Emphasis on beauty, style, sensation, and formal qualities.

* Resistance to Victorian moralism and utilitarianism.

* Cultivation of refined taste and artistic autonomy.

* Overlap with Decadence, although the movements are not identical.

Major writers and works

* Walter Pater: Studies in the History of the Renaissance

* Oscar Wilde: The Picture of Dorian Gray, The Importance of Being Earnest

* Algernon Charles Swinburne: Poems and Ballads

* James McNeill Whistler: Important visual-art figure associated with aesthetic principles.

Key critical association: Walter Pater’s writing strongly influenced aesthetic sensibility; Wilde became its best-known literary spokesperson.

UGC NET tip: “Art for art’s sake” is the phrase to associate with Aestheticism. Wilde’s The Picture of Dorian Gray explores the tension between aesthetic values, moral responsibility, and consequences.

## 11. Decadence

Period: Mainly late 19th century, especially the 1880s–1890s

Definition: Decadence is a literary and artistic tendency associated with cultivated artificiality, refined sensation, stylistic excess, and a fascination with decline, taboo, and moral or social transgression.

Key features

* Artificiality valued over ordinary nature.

* Sensuousness, exoticism, and elaborate style.

* Interest in taboo, ennui, corruption, and degeneration.

* Rejection of conventional moral and social expectations.

* Overlap with Symbolism and Aestheticism.

Major writers and works

* Charles Baudelaire: The Flowers of Evil (Les Fleurs du mal)

* Joris-Karl Huysmans: Against Nature (À rebours, 1884)

* Oscar Wilde: The Picture of Dorian Gray

* Aubrey Beardsley: Important illustrator associated with the Decadent aesthetic.

UGC NET tip: Huysmans’s Against Nature is a key Decadent text. Aestheticism stresses the autonomy of art and beauty; Decadence more specifically explores artificiality, excess, ennui, and transgression.

## 12. Realism

Period: Mainly 19th century; developed at different times across national literatures

Definition: Realism seeks to represent ordinary life, social relationships, and recognizable human experience with credible detail rather than idealization.

Key features

* Everyday settings and ordinary characters.

* Plausible events, motivation, and social interaction.

* Attention to class, money, marriage, work, and institutions.

* Detailed description and psychologically convincing characterization.

* Social criticism without necessarily abandoning narrative coherence.

Major writers and works

* George Eliot: Middlemarch

* Charles Dickens: Bleak House

* William Makepeace Thackeray: Vanity Fair

* Gustave Flaubert: Madame Bovary

* Honoré de Balzac: Père Goriot

* Leo Tolstoy: Anna Karenina

* Anton Chekhov: Short stories and plays

UGC NET tip: Realism represents life plausibly; it does not mean that a literary work is a completely unmediated copy of reality.

## 13. Naturalism

Period: Mainly late 19th century, extending into the early 20th century

Definition: Naturalism is a development of realism that emphasizes how heredity, environment, social conditions, and material circumstances shape human behavior.

Key features

* Influence of scientific thought and determinism.

* Individuals shaped by heredity and environment.

* Attention to poverty, sexuality, violence, illness, and social pressures.

* Characters often struggle against forces beyond their control.

* Detailed, sometimes stark depictions of life.

Major writers and works

* Émile Zola: Germinal, Thérèse Raquin

* Stephen Crane: Maggie: A Girl of the Streets, The Red Badge of Courage

* Theodore Dreiser: Sister Carrie

* Thomas Hardy: Tess of the d’Urbervilles and Jude the Obscure contain important naturalistic tendencies.

UGC NET tip: Zola is a central theorist and practitioner of Naturalism. Realism broadly represents ordinary life; Naturalism gives particular emphasis to deterministic forces such as heredity and environment.

## 14. Modernism

Period: Mainly late 19th century to the mid-20th century; especially influential c. 1910–1945

Definition: Modernism is a broad literary and artistic movement that experiments with form and language to represent modernity, fragmentation, psychological complexity, and the crisis of inherited values.

Key features

* Fragmented structure and discontinuous chronology.

* Stream of consciousness and interior monologue.

* Multiple perspectives and unreliable narration.

* Mythic allusion and formal experimentation.

* Alienation, urban life, war, and cultural crisis.

* Rejection of straightforward Victorian narrative conventions.

Major writers and works

* James Joyce: Ulysses, A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man

* Virginia Woolf: Mrs Dalloway, To the Lighthouse

* T. S. Eliot: The Waste Land, “The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock”

* Ezra Pound: The Cantos

* W. B. Yeats: “The Second Coming”

* D. H. Lawrence: Sons and Lovers

* Joseph Conrad: Heart of Darkness

* William Faulkner: The Sound and the Fury

Critical associations

* T. S. Eliot: tradition, impersonality, and the objective correlative.

* Ezra Pound: “Make it new.”

* Virginia Woolf: experiments in consciousness and subjective time.

* James Joyce: stream of consciousness and mythic parallels.

UGC NET tip: Modernism is not a single technique. Fragmentation, formal innovation, and the representation of consciousness are recurring tendencies, not universal requirements.

## 15. Imagism

Period: Early 20th century, especially 1910s

Definition: Imagism was a poetic movement advocating precision, economy of language, and the direct presentation of an image.

Key features

* Direct treatment of the subject.

* Economy and precision of diction.

* Avoidance of unnecessary words.

* Musical rhythm rather than mechanical metrical regularity.

* Use of concrete, sharply defined images.

Major poets and works

* Ezra Pound: “In a Station of the Metro”

* H. D. (Hilda Doolittle): “Oread”

* Richard Aldington: Images

* Amy Lowell: Some Imagist Poets anthologies

Key critical document: Ezra Pound’s “A Few Don’ts by an Imagiste” (1913).

UGC NET tip: Pound’s “In a Station of the Metro” is a signature Imagist poem. Its concise presentation of faces and petals demonstrates the movement’s emphasis on compression and image.

## 16. Symbolism

Period: Late 19th century, originating especially in France

Definition: Symbolism is a literary movement that uses suggestive images, symbols, musical language, and indirect expression to evoke ideas and states of consciousness that cannot easily be stated directly.

Key features

* Suggestion rather than direct explanation.

* Symbols with layered or ambiguous meanings.

* Musicality and sound patterns.

* Dreams, mystery, spirituality, and subjective experience.

* Associations between different senses and perceptions.

Major writers and works

* Charles Baudelaire: “Correspondences,” The Flowers of Evil

* Stéphane Mallarmé: “The Afternoon of a Faun”

* Paul Verlaine: Romances sans paroles

* Arthur Rimbaud: Illuminations

* W. B. Yeats: “The Second Coming,” “Sailing to Byzantium” (important later symbolic poetry)

UGC NET tip: French Symbolism influenced modernist poetry. Do not confuse Symbolism with the mere presence of a symbol: it is a movement that emphasizes suggestion, ambiguity, and musical language.

## 17. Vorticism

Period: Britain, c. 1914–1915

Definition: Vorticism was a short-lived avant-garde movement that combined elements of Cubism and Futurism while emphasizing concentrated energy, angular form, and dynamic modernity.

Key features

* Geometric, angular, and abstract forms.

* Interest in the energy of modern urban and industrial life.

* Rejection of sentimental and conventional representation.

* Connection between visual art and experimental writing.

* Emphasis on the “vortex” as a concentrated center of energy.

Major figures and works

* Wyndham Lewis: Tarr; editor of the journal BLAST.

* Ezra Pound: Associated with Vorticist theory and advocacy.

* Henri Gaudier-Brzeska: Sculptor associated with the movement.

Key publication: BLAST (1914), the principal Vorticist magazine.

UGC NET tip: Vorticism is strongly associated with Wyndham Lewis and BLAST. It is a visual-art and literary avant-garde movement, not simply another name for Imagism.

## 18. Expressionism

Period: Especially c. 1905–1920s; influential in German-language art and drama

Definition: Expressionism distorts external reality to communicate intense subjective emotion, anxiety, alienation, or psychological experience.

Key features

* Exaggerated or distorted forms and situations.

* Subjective rather than photographic representation.

* Alienation, fear, social crisis, and inner conflict.

* Fragmented scenes and stylized dialogue in drama.

* Characters may function as types rather than fully individualized realist figures.

Major writers and works

* Georg Kaiser: From Morn to Midnight

* Ernst Toller: Man and the Masses

* August Strindberg: A Dream Play (a precursor and influence on expressionistic drama)

* Franz Kafka: The Metamorphosis shares expressionistic concerns, though Kafka should not be classified exclusively as an Expressionist.

UGC NET tip: Expressionism foregrounds subjective emotional truth through distortion. In theatre, it often opposes naturalistic staging and characterization.

## 19. Surrealism

Period: Emerged in the 1920s, especially in France

Definition: Surrealism sought to release creative expression from the constraints of conscious rational control, drawing on dreams, the unconscious, unexpected juxtapositions, and psychoanalytic ideas.

Key features

* Dream logic and irrational combinations.

* Free association and automatic writing.

* Unexpected images and shocking juxtapositions.

* Interest in the unconscious and desire.

* Challenge to conventional distinctions between reality and fantasy.

Major writers and works

* André Breton: Manifesto of Surrealism (1924), Nadja

* Paul Éluard: Capitale de la douleur

* Louis Aragon: Paris Peasant

* Antonin Artaud: Associated with the avant-garde milieu; his Theatre of Cruelty is a related but distinct project.

Key influence: Sigmund Freud’s theories of dreams and the unconscious.

UGC NET tip: André Breton is the central name associated with the Surrealist manifesto. Surrealism uses dreamlike combinations, whereas Dada is particularly associated with anti-art protest and the rejection of established cultural values.

## 20. Dada

Period: Began in 1916 during the First World War; developed across European and American avant-garde circles

Definition: Dada was an anti-establishment avant-garde movement that challenged conventional art, rationality, nationalism, and the cultural values associated with the First World War.

Key features

* Absurdity, chance, collage, and fragmentation.

* Rejection of conventional artistic standards.

* Provocation and deliberate nonsense.

* Criticism of bourgeois culture and claims of rational progress.

* Performance, sound poetry, and experimental visual art.

Major figures and works

* Tristan Tzara: Seven Dada Manifestos

* Hugo Ball: Sound-poetry performances at the Cabaret Voltaire.

* Hannah Höch: Photomontage works.

* Marcel Duchamp: Fountain (1917), a major anti-art provocation.

* Richard Huelsenbeck: Important Dada writer and organizer.

Key place: Cabaret Voltaire, Zurich, opened in 1916.

UGC NET tip: Dada’s defining attitude is anti-art and anti-establishment protest. Surrealism later developed a more sustained program focused on dreams and the unconscious.

## 21. Postmodernism

Period: Especially after the Second World War; highly influential from the 1960s onward

Definition: Postmodernism is a broad cultural and literary tendency that questions stable meanings, unified identities, authoritative narratives, and clear boundaries between high and popular culture.

Key features

* Metafiction: Fiction that draws attention to its own constructed nature.

* Historiographic metafiction: Fiction that questions how history is narrated.

* Parody, pastiche, and intertextuality.

* Fragmentation and self-reflexivity.

* Blurring of fact and fiction, high and popular culture.

* Skepticism toward universal explanations or “grand narratives.”

Major writers and works

* John Fowles: The French Lieutenant’s Woman

* Thomas Pynchon: The Crying of Lot 49

* Kurt Vonnegut: Slaughterhouse-Five

* Italo Calvino: If on a winter’s night a traveler

* Salman Rushdie: Midnight’s Children

* Jeanette Winterson: Oranges Are Not the Only Fruit

Key theorists

* Jean-François Lyotard: The Postmodern Condition; skepticism toward metanarratives.

* Jean Baudrillard: Simulacra and simulation.

* Linda Hutcheon: Historiographic metafiction and postmodern poetics.

* Fredric Jameson: Postmodernism as the cultural logic of late capitalism.

UGC NET tip: Modernism often searches for order amid fragmentation, while Postmodernism frequently questions whether a single authoritative order can be established at all. This is a useful distinction, not an absolute rule.

## 22. Postcolonialism

Period: Emerged as a major critical field in the mid-to-late 20th century; especially influential from the 1970s onward

Definition: Postcolonialism examines the cultural, political, linguistic, and psychological effects of colonialism and the ways colonized peoples resist, negotiate, or transform colonial power.

Key features

* Colonial representation and the construction of the “Other.”

* Identity, hybridity, diaspora, and cultural displacement.

* Language, power, and the colonial education system.

* Resistance, nationalism, and decolonization.

* Recovery and reinterpretation of suppressed histories.

Major theorists and works

* Edward Said: Orientalism (1978), Culture and Imperialism

* Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak: “Can the Subaltern Speak?”

* Homi K. Bhabha: The Location of Culture

* Frantz Fanon: The Wretched of the Earth, Black Skin, White Masks

* Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o: Decolonising the Mind

* Chinua Achebe: Things Fall Apart

* Salman Rushdie: Midnight’s Children

* Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie: Half of a Yellow Sun

Important concepts

* Orientalism: Said’s analysis of Western knowledge and representation of the East as an object of power.

* Hybridity: Cultural identities and forms produced through contact and unequal colonial relations.

* Mimicry: Bhabha’s concept of the colonized subject’s partial imitation of colonial norms, which can unsettle colonial authority.

* Subaltern: Groups excluded from dominant structures of power and representation.

UGC NET tip: Said = Orientalism; Spivak = “Can the Subaltern Speak?”; Bhabha = hybridity, mimicry, and ambivalence; Fanon = colonial psychology and decolonization.

## 23. Feminism

Period: Intellectual roots extend much earlier; modern feminist movements developed in several waves from the 18th century onward

Definition: Feminism examines gender inequality, patriarchy, and the social, political, and cultural structures that shape women’s lives and representation.

Key features

* Critique of patriarchal power and gender roles.

* Recovery of neglected women writers.

* Analysis of gender in language, literature, and culture.

* Attention to sexuality, class, race, labor, and identity.

* Challenges to the literary canon and assumptions about authorship.

Major theorists and works

* Mary Wollstonecraft: A Vindication of the Rights of Woman

* Virginia Woolf: A Room of One’s Own

* Simone de Beauvoir: The Second Sex

* Kate Millett: Sexual Politics

* Elaine Showalter: A Literature of Their Own

* Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar: The Madwoman in the Attic

* bell hooks: Ain’t I a Woman?

* Judith Butler: Gender Trouble

Important concepts

* Patriarchy: Social systems in which men disproportionately hold power.

* Gynocriticism: Showalter’s term for studying women as writers, their literary traditions, and their creative practices.

* Gender performativity: Butler’s account of how gender is constituted through repeated acts and social conventions.

* Intersectionality: Kimberlé Crenshaw’s term for overlapping structures of discrimination and power.

UGC NET tip: Woolf = A Room of One’s Own; Showalter = gynocriticism; Gilbert and Gubar = The Madwoman in the Attic; Butler = gender performativity; Crenshaw = intersectionality.

## 24. Marxism

Period: Based on 19th-century Marxist thought; developed into major literary-critical traditions in the 20th century

Definition: Marxist literary criticism studies literature in relation to material conditions, class relations, ideology, labor, and the organization of society.

Key features

* Class struggle and economic inequality.

* Relationship between literary production and social conditions.

* Ideology and the reproduction of dominant values.

* Commodification, labor, and capitalism.

* Literature’s capacity to reinforce or challenge existing power structures.

Major theorists and works

* Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels: The Communist Manifesto

* Georg Lukács: The Theory of the Novel

* Antonio Gramsci: Selections from the Prison Notebooks

* Louis Althusser: “Ideology and Ideological State Apparatuses”

* Raymond Williams: Marxism and Literature

* Terry Eagleton: Marxism and Literary Criticism

* Fredric Jameson: The Political Unconscious

Important concepts

* Base and superstructure: A model relating material and economic organization to political, legal, and cultural institutions.

* Ideology: Ideas and representations that help organize or legitimize social relations.

* Hegemony: Gramsci’s concept of leadership and dominance maintained partly through consent.

* Reification: The treatment of social relations as though they were things.

* Commodification: The transformation of goods, practices, or relations into commodities.

UGC NET tip: Gramsci = hegemony; Althusser = ideological state apparatuses; Williams = cultural materialism; Jameson = political unconscious.

## 25. Structuralism

Period: Developed from early 20th-century linguistics; influential in literary criticism during the 1950s–1970s

Definition: Structuralism studies the underlying systems, conventions, and relations that make meaning possible in language, literature, and culture.

Key features

* Meaning emerges through relationships within a system.

* Literature is studied through conventions, codes, and structures.

* Linguistic models influence literary analysis.

* Narratives can be analyzed through recurring patterns and functions.

* Individual texts are considered within broader systems of signification.

Major theorists and works

* Ferdinand de Saussure: Course in General Linguistics

* Claude Lévi-Strauss: Structural Anthropology

* Roland Barthes: Mythologies, S/Z

* Vladimir Propp: Morphology of the Folktale

* Tzvetan Todorov: The Poetics of Prose

* Gérard Genette: Narrative Discourse

Important concepts

* Signifier: The form of a sign, such as a spoken or written word.

* Signified: The concept associated with that form.

* Langue: The underlying system of a language.

* Parole: An individual act of language use.

* Binary opposition: A pair of contrasting terms that organizes meaning, such as nature/culture.

* Mytheme: A basic unit in the structural analysis of myth, associated with Lévi-Strauss.

UGC NET tip: Saussure provides the linguistic foundation; Propp analyzes folktale structures; Lévi-Strauss applies structural analysis to myth and culture.

## 26. Post-structuralism

Period: Mainly 1960s onward

Definition: Post-structuralism questions the idea that language and cultural systems provide completely stable structures of meaning.

Key features

* Meaning is unstable, contextual, and relational.

* Texts may undermine their own apparent arguments.

* Challenges to fixed binary oppositions.

* Skepticism toward unified subjects and final interpretations.

* Attention to contradictions, exclusions, and differences.

Major theorists and works

* Jacques Derrida: Of Grammatology

* Michel Foucault: The Archaeology of Knowledge, Discipline and Punish

* Roland Barthes: “The Death of the Author”

* Julia Kristeva: Desire in Language

* Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari: A Thousand Plateaus

Important concepts

* Deconstruction: Derrida’s approach to exposing tensions and hierarchies within texts and concepts.

* Différance: Derrida’s term highlighting both difference and the deferral of meaning.

* Death of the Author: Barthes’s challenge to treating authorial intention as the final authority on textual meaning.

* Discourse: In Foucault, systems of statements and practices that organize knowledge and power.

UGC NET tip: Structuralism seeks to identify the systems underlying meaning; post-structuralism questions the stability, completeness, and authority of those systems.

## 27. New Historicism

Period: Emerged in the late 1970s and 1980s, especially in American literary criticism

Definition: New Historicism reads literary texts in relation to the historical discourses, institutions, and power relations that shape both literature and its context.

Key features

* Literature and history are mutually implicated.

* Texts are read alongside non-literary documents.

* Power operates through everyday practices and cultural representations.

* Challenges the idea of literature as autonomous from history.

* Examines how historical meanings are produced and circulated.

Major theorists and works

* Stephen Greenblatt: Renaissance Self-Fashioning

* Louis Montrose: “Professing the Renaissance”

* Catherine Gallagher: Practicing New Historicism (with Stephen Greenblatt)

* Michel Foucault: Major intellectual influence, particularly his work on discourse and power.

Important concepts

* Historicity of texts: Texts are shaped by their historical conditions.

* Textuality of history: Historical knowledge is accessed through representations and surviving documents.

* Power and discourse: Cultural practices help produce and regulate knowledge and identity.

UGC NET tip: Greenblatt is the central name associated with New Historicism. Foucault is a major influence, not the founder of New Historicism.

## 28. Cultural Materialism

Period: Developed in Britain, especially during the 1980s

Definition: Cultural Materialism is a form of literary and cultural criticism that studies texts through their material conditions of production, historical circumstances, institutions, and ideological effects, often with an explicitly political emphasis.

Key features

* Strong connection between literature and political power.

* Reinterpretation of canonical texts from marginalized perspectives.

* Attention to class, gender, race, and institutional control.

* Emphasis on historical struggles and cultural production.

* Challenges supposedly neutral or timeless interpretations of literature.

Major theorists and works

* Raymond Williams: Marxism and Literature, Culture and Society

* Jonathan Dollimore: Radical Tragedy

* Alan Sinfield: Faultlines

* Catherine Belsey: Critical Practice

Important distinction: Cultural Materialism and New Historicism both connect literature with history and power. Cultural Materialism is often more explicitly left-wing and politically interventionist, while New Historicism is particularly associated with the analysis of cultural circulation and power.

UGC NET tip: Raymond Williams is a foundational figure in cultural materialist thought; Dollimore and Sinfield are key names in British literary Cultural Materialism.

## 29. Reader-response criticism

Period: Particularly influential from the 1960s onward

Definition: Reader-response criticism argues that reading is an active process in which meaning emerges through the interaction between a text and its reader.

Key features

* The reader participates in producing meaning.

* Interpretation can vary with readers, contexts, and reading strategies.

* Attention to expectations, gaps, and the reading process.

* Challenges the assumption that a text possesses one entirely self-contained meaning.

* Different theorists assign different degrees of freedom to readers.

Major theorists and works

* Wolfgang Iser: The Implied Reader, The Act of Reading

* Stanley Fish: Is There a Text in This Class?

* Hans Robert Jauss: Toward an Aesthetic of Reception

* Norman Holland: The Dynamics of Literary Response

* Louise Rosenblatt: Literature as Exploration

Important concepts

* Implied reader: Iser’s term for the reader-role anticipated by a text.

* Gaps or blanks: Unspecified elements that readers help complete.

* Interpretive communities: Fish’s term for communities whose shared conventions shape interpretation.

* Horizon of expectations: Jauss’s concept for the historically shaped expectations through which readers approach works.

UGC NET tip: Iser = implied reader and textual gaps; Fish = interpretive communities; Jauss = reception history and horizon of expectations.

## 30. Ecocriticism

Period: Emerged as a modern literary-critical field in the late 20th century; expanded significantly from the 1990s

Definition: Ecocriticism studies the relationship between literature, culture, and the physical environment, including the ways texts represent nature, animals, landscapes, climate, and ecological crises.

Key features

* Nature and environment as significant subjects of literary analysis.

* Critique of anthropocentrism, or human-centered thinking.

* Attention to environmental justice and unequal ecological burdens.

* Analysis of pastoral, wilderness, landscape, and climate narratives.

* Exploration of human and nonhuman relationships.

Major theorists and works

* William Rueckert: “Literature and Ecology: An Experiment in Ecocriticism”

* Cheryll Glotfelty: The Ecocriticism Reader (co-edited with Harold Fromm)

* Lawrence Buell: The Environmental Imagination

* Greg Garrard: Ecocriticism

* Rob Nixon: Slow Violence and the Environmentalism of the Poor

* Donna Haraway: Important contributor to multispecies and posthumanist thought.

Important concepts

* Anthropocentrism: Treating human interests as the primary measure of value.

* Deep ecology: An ecological philosophy associated with the intrinsic value of nonhuman life.

* Environmental justice: The relationship between ecological harm and social inequality.

* Slow violence: Nixon’s term for gradual, dispersed environmental harm that is difficult to see as a single event.

UGC NET tip: Glotfelty is strongly associated with establishing ecocriticism as a literary-critical field; Buell is important for environmental literary studies; Nixon is associated with slow violence.

## 31. Queer theory

Period: Emerged as a recognizable academic field in the early 1990s

Definition: Queer theory examines how sexuality, gender, and identity are socially and culturally constructed, questioning categories that appear fixed, natural, or universally applicable.

Key features

* Critique of compulsory heterosexuality and heteronormativity.

* Analysis of the relationship between sexuality, gender, identity, and power.

* Challenges to fixed categories of male/female and heterosexual/homosexual.

* Attention to ambiguity, fluidity, and non-normative identities.

* Reinterpretation of literary characters, genres, and cultural conventions.

Major theorists and works

* Judith Butler: Gender Trouble

* Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick: Between Men, Epistemology of the Closet

* Michel Foucault: The History of Sexuality, Volume 1

* Teresa de Lauretis: Associated with the early academic use of the term “queer theory.”

* Adrienne Rich: “Compulsory Heterosexuality and Lesbian Existence”

Important concepts

* Heteronormativity: The assumption that heterosexuality is normal or standard.

* Gender performativity: Butler’s argument that gender is constituted through repeated acts and conventions.

* Compulsory heterosexuality: Rich’s critique of the social enforcement of heterosexuality.

* Closet: Sedgwick’s important framework for understanding the cultural organization of sexual knowledge and disclosure.

UGC NET tip: Butler = Gender Trouble; Sedgwick = Epistemology of the Closet; Foucault = history of sexuality and the relation between power and knowledge.

## 32. Dalit literature

Period: Roots extend earlier; modern Dalit literary movements became especially prominent in the 20th century

Definition: Dalit literature is writing that represents, critiques, and resists caste oppression, often foregrounding the lived experiences, dignity, resistance, and self-representation of Dalit communities.

Key features

* Critique of caste hierarchy and untouchability.

* Assertion of dignity, identity, and social justice.

* Testimony, autobiography, poetry, fiction, and life writing.

* Rejection of caste-based exclusion from literary and cultural institutions.

* Emphasis on lived experience, collective memory, and political resistance.

Major writers and works

* B. R. Ambedkar: Annihilation of Caste; a foundational anti-caste text.

* Omprakash Valmiki: Joothan.

* Daya Pawar: Baluta.

* Sharan Kumar Limbale: Akkarmashi (The Outcaste), Towards an Aesthetic of Dalit Literature.

* Bama: Karukku.

* Namdeo Dhasal: Golpitha.

* Baby Kamble: The Prisons We Broke.

* Urmila Pawar: The Weave of My Life.

Important concepts

* Caste oppression: A hierarchical system of social exclusion and inherited status.

* Testimonial writing: Narratives that record lived experience and social injustice.

* Dalit aesthetics: Debates about literary form, political commitment, experience, and resistance.

* Self-representation: The importance of marginalized communities articulating their own experiences rather than being represented solely by dominant groups.

UGC NET tip: Joothan by Omprakash Valmiki, Baluta by Daya Pawar, and Karukku by Bama are key autobiographical texts. Ambedkar is central to anti-caste thought, though his work is not limited to literary criticism.

## 33. Subaltern Studies

Period: Emerged in South Asian historiography in the early 1980s

Definition: Subaltern Studies is a historiographical and critical project that examines the experiences and political agency of groups marginalized by dominant colonial, nationalist, and elite historical narratives.

Key features

* Critique of elite-centered histories.

* Recovery of marginalized and subordinated groups’ agency.

* Analysis of colonialism, nationalism, class, caste, and power.

* Questioning who is authorized to represent the oppressed.

* Later connections with postcolonial theory and debates about representation.

Major thinkers and works

* Ranajit Guha: Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India.

* Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak: “Can the Subaltern Speak?”

* Dipesh Chakrabarty: Provincializing Europe.

* Partha Chatterjee: Nationalist Thought and the Colonial World.

* Shahid Amin: Event, Metaphor, Memory.

Important concepts

* Subaltern: A person or group marginalized from dominant political and social structures. The term derives from Antonio Gramsci’s writings.

* Elite historiography: Historical narratives that privilege ruling, colonial, or nationalist elites.

* Representation: Both speaking on behalf of others and depicting them; Spivak examines the complications of these forms of representation.

* Agency: The capacity of marginalized people to act, resist, and shape historical events.

UGC NET tip: Ranajit Guha is the founder associated with the Subaltern Studies collective. Spivak’s “Can the Subaltern Speak?” is a landmark theoretical essay, but Subaltern Studies is not identical to postcolonialism as a whole.

## Crucial distinctions for UGC NET

| Commonly confused terms                  | Main difference                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Renaissance vs. Humanism                 | Renaissance is a broad cultural revival; Humanism is a major intellectual orientation within it.                                                                                            |
| Restoration vs. Neoclassicism            | Restoration begins with the monarchy’s return in 1660; Neoclassicism is a broader literary aesthetic extending through much of the 18th century.                                            |
| Realism vs. Naturalism                   | Realism emphasizes plausible everyday life; Naturalism stresses environmental, hereditary, and material determinants.                                                                       |
| Aestheticism vs. Decadence               | Aestheticism emphasizes art’s autonomy and beauty; Decadence emphasizes artificiality, excess, ennui, and transgression.                                                                    |
| Modernism vs. Postmodernism              | Modernism experiments with form in response to cultural fragmentation; Postmodernism often questions the possibility of a single stable order or authoritative narrative.                   |
| Imagism vs. Symbolism                    | Imagism emphasizes precise, economical images; Symbolism emphasizes suggestion, layered meanings, and musicality.                                                                           |
| Dada vs. Surrealism                      | Dada foregrounds anti-art protest and absurdity; Surrealism emphasizes dreams and the unconscious.                                                                                          |
| Structuralism vs. Post-structuralism     | Structuralism studies systems that produce meaning; Post-structuralism questions their stability and closure.                                                                               |
| New Historicism vs. Cultural Materialism | Both connect literature and power; Cultural Materialism is often more explicitly politically interventionist.                                                                               |
| Feminism vs. Queer theory                | Feminism examines gendered power and inequality; Queer theory especially interrogates the construction and regulation of sexuality and gender categories.                                   |
| Postcolonialism vs. Subaltern Studies    | Postcolonialism broadly examines colonial power and its legacies; Subaltern Studies grew from a more specific project of recovering marginalized groups’ agency in South Asian history.     |
| Dalit literature vs. Subaltern Studies   | Dalit literature is a body of writing and resistance rooted in caste experience; Subaltern Studies is a critical-historiographical project concerned with marginalized groups more broadly. |

Final revision strategy: Prioritize the movement’s approximate period, one defining feature, two major names, and one signature work. For critical theories, memorize the theorist–concept–book connection; these associations are especially useful for matching and chronology questions in UGC NET.
