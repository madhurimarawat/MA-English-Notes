# 📚 UGC NET English: Poetry — Detailed Notes with Examples

These are important poetic forms, genres, and verse techniques for UGC NET English Literature. For each term, learn its definition, key features, famous examples, and the writers associated with it.

## 1. 💌 Sonnet

Definition: A sonnet is a poem of 14 lines, traditionally written in iambic pentameter in English.

There are two major types:

A. Petrarchan (Italian) Sonnet

* 14 lines divided into an octave (8 lines) and a sestet (6 lines).

* The octave commonly follows the rhyme scheme ABBA ABBA.

* The sestet often follows CDE CDE or CDC DCD.

* The volta, or turn in thought, often occurs between the octave and sestet.

📖 Example: John Milton's “When I Consider How My Light Is Spent.”

B. Shakespearean (English) Sonnet

* Three quatrains (four lines each) followed by a final rhyming couplet.

* Typical rhyme scheme: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG.

* Often written in iambic pentameter.

📖 Example: Shakespeare's Sonnet 18, “Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?”

🎯 UGC NET tip: Remember Shakespeare, Petrarch, 14 lines, volta, and the distinction between octave-sestet and three quatrains plus a couplet.

## 2. 🌹 Ode

Definition: An ode is a formal lyric poem that celebrates, praises, or deeply reflects on a person, object, idea, or experience.

Types of ode:

* Pindaric ode: Inspired by the Greek poet Pindar; traditionally structured in strophe, antistrophe, and epode.

* Horatian ode: Usually more personal, reflective, and regular in structure.

* Irregular ode: Does not follow a fixed classical stanzaic pattern.

📖 Famous examples:

* John Keats, “Ode to a Nightingale”

* John Keats, “Ode on a Grecian Urn”

* P. B. Shelley, “Ode to the West Wind”

* William Wordsworth, “Ode: Intimations of Immortality”

🎯 UGC NET tip: Keats is strongly associated with the Romantic ode. Shelley’s “Ode to the West Wind” has five sections, each consisting of 14 lines.

## 3. 🥀 Elegy

Definition: An elegy is a poem of mourning, loss, or meditation on death. It may also reflect on mortality, memory, and consolation.

📖 Famous examples:

* Thomas Gray, Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard

* John Milton, Lycidas, written on the death of Edward King

* P. B. Shelley, Adonais, written on the death of John Keats

* W. H. Auden, “In Memory of W. B. Yeats”

Important distinction:

* A pastoral elegy presents grief through the conventions of pastoral poetry, often using shepherds and an idealised countryside.

* Lycidas is a pastoral elegy.

* Adonais is also a pastoral elegy.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Memorise the poet, poem, and person mourned, especially Milton and Edward King, Shelley and Keats.

## 4. ⚔️ Epic

Definition: An epic is a long narrative poem dealing with the heroic deeds of a great figure, a nation's origins, or events of major cultural significance.

Common features:

* Invocation of a Muse

* Beginning in medias res, meaning in the middle of the action

* Epic similes

* Supernatural beings or divine intervention

* Heroic journeys, battles, and elevated language

* Catalogues of warriors, ships, or places

📖 Famous examples:

* Homer's Iliad and Odyssey

* Virgil's Aeneid

* John Milton's Paradise Lost

* Edmund Spenser's The Faerie Queene

Types:

* Primary or oral epic: Associated with oral tradition, such as Homer's epics.

* Secondary or literary epic: Deliberately composed in a literary tradition, such as Paradise Lost.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Milton's Paradise Lost begins with an invocation and begins its main action after the fall of Satan, using the technique of in medias res.

## 5. 🎶 Ballad

Definition: A ballad is a narrative poem that traditionally tells a story through simple language, dialogue, dramatic events, and musical rhythm.

Typical features:

* Quatrains are common.

* The second and fourth lines often rhyme.

* Repetition and refrains may occur.

* The story may involve love, tragedy, supernatural events, or heroic deeds.

📖 Famous examples:

* “Sir Patrick Spens,” a traditional ballad

* “Barbara Allen,” a traditional ballad

* Samuel Taylor Coleridge, The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

* John Keats, “La Belle Dame sans Merci”

Types of ballad:

* Folk ballad: Traditionally transmitted orally, often with uncertain authorship.

* Literary ballad: Written by a known author in imitation of ballad traditions.

🎯 UGC NET tip: The ballad stanza commonly alternates four-stress and three-stress lines, often with an ABCB rhyme scheme. This is a common pattern, not an absolute rule.

## 6. 💖 Lyric

Definition: A lyric is a relatively short poem that expresses personal feelings, thoughts, emotions, or reflections.

Originally, lyric poetry was associated with musical accompaniment.

Features:

* Emphasis on emotion and subjective experience

* Often uses a first-person speaker

* May focus on love, nature, memory, faith, or mortality

* Usually shorter than an epic or extended narrative poem

📖 Famous examples:

* William Wordsworth, “I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud”

* John Keats, “Ode to a Nightingale”

* William Blake, “The Tyger”

* Shakespeare's Sonnets

🎯 UGC NET tip: A sonnet or ode can also be a lyric. The categories overlap because lyric describes a broad kind of poetry, while sonnet and ode identify more specific forms or genres.

## 7. 🌿 Villanelle

Definition: A villanelle is a 19-line poem consisting of five tercets (three-line stanzas) followed by a quatrain (four-line stanza).

Main features:

* 19 lines in total

* Two repeating refrains

* Two rhyme sounds throughout, conventionally ABA

* The first and third lines of the opening stanza recur alternately as refrains.

* Both refrains appear together in the final quatrain.

📖 Famous example: Dylan Thomas, “Do not go gentle into that good night.”

The repeated lines “Do not go gentle into that good night” and “Rage, rage against the dying of the light” create urgency and emotional intensity.

Another example is Elizabeth Bishop's “One Art.”

🎯 UGC NET tip: Villanelle = 19 lines + 5 tercets + 1 quatrain + 2 refrains.

## 8. 🔄 Sestina

Definition: A sestina is a highly structured poem of six six-line stanzas followed by a three-line envoi, also called a tornada.

Main features:

* Six stanzas of six lines each

* Six end-words recur in a prescribed rotating order

* A final three-line envoi uses the six end-words again

* The form depends on repetition of end-words rather than a conventional rhyme scheme

📖 Famous example: Elizabeth Bishop, “Sestina.”

Bishop's poem repeats words associated with domestic life, including “house,” “grandmother,” and “child,” creating connections between memory, routine, and emotion.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Sestina = 6 × 6-line stanzas + 3-line envoi = 39 lines.

## 9. 🔗 Terza Rima

Definition: Terza rima is a verse form using interlocking three-line stanzas, usually with the rhyme scheme ABA BCB CDC DED.

Each middle line's rhyme links one tercet to the next.

📖 Famous examples:

* Dante Alighieri, The Divine Comedy

* P. B. Shelley, “Ode to the West Wind”

* Robert Frost, “Acquainted with the Night”

In “Ode to the West Wind,” the interlocking rhyme helps create a continuous forward movement.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Terza rima = interlocking tercets, usually ABA BCB CDC. Dante is the key association.

## 10. 📏 Blank Verse

Definition: Blank verse is unrhymed verse written in a regular metre, most commonly iambic pentameter in English literature.

An iamb generally consists of an unstressed syllable followed by a stressed syllable. Pentameter usually has five metrical feet per line.

📖 Famous examples:

* Christopher Marlowe, Doctor Faustus

* William Shakespeare, Hamlet and other plays

* John Milton, Paradise Lost

* William Wordsworth, The Prelude

Example of the rhythm:

To be | or not | to be | that is | the question.

This is a familiar teaching illustration of iambic-pentameter rhythm, although actual dramatic delivery and scansion may vary.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Blank verse is not free verse. Blank verse has a regular metre but no regular end rhyme.

## 11. 🕊️ Free Verse

Definition: Free verse is poetry that does not follow a fixed, regular metre or a prescribed rhyme scheme.

It may still use rhythm, repetition, sound patterns, line breaks, and other forms of structure.

📖 Famous examples:

* Walt Whitman, Leaves of Grass

* T. S. Eliot, The Waste Land

* Ezra Pound, The Cantos

* W. H. Auden, “The Shield of Achilles”

🎯 UGC NET tip: Free verse does not mean the absence of all structure. Its rhythms and patterns are often created through syntax, sound, imagery, and lineation.

## 12. 🤝 Heroic Couplet

Definition: A heroic couplet consists of two successive rhyming lines, usually written in iambic pentameter.

Main features:

* Two-line units

* End rhyme, usually AA

* Iambic pentameter

* Often a self-contained grammatical or rhetorical unit

📖 Famous examples:

* Geoffrey Chaucer, The Canterbury Tales

* Alexander Pope, The Rape of the Lock

* Alexander Pope, An Essay on Criticism

* John Dryden, Absalom and Achitophel

Example:

True wit is nature to advantage dressed;

What oft was thought, but ne'er so well expressed.

These lines are from Alexander Pope's An Essay on Criticism.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Heroic couplets are strongly associated with Neoclassical poetry, especially Dryden and Pope. Chaucer also used rhyming couplets, though his metre differs from the standard later heroic couplet.

## 13. 🎭 Dramatic Monologue

Definition: A dramatic monologue is a poem in which a single speaker addresses a silent or implied listener at a particular dramatic moment, revealing the speaker's character, motives, and psychology.

Three essential features:

1. A single identifiable speaker.

2. An implied listener or audience.

3. The speaker unintentionally or indirectly reveals important aspects of their character.

📖 Famous examples:

* Robert Browning, “My Last Duchess”

* Robert Browning, “Porphyria's Lover”

* Alfred, Lord Tennyson, “Ulysses”

* Robert Browning, “The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church”

In “My Last Duchess,” the Duke describes a portrait of his former wife to a visitor. While attempting to present himself as powerful and refined, he reveals his jealousy and controlling nature.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Robert Browning is the central figure to remember. Dramatic monologue is not merely any poem that contains a speaker talking.

## 14. 🐑 Pastoral

Definition: Pastoral is a literary genre that represents rural life, shepherds, and the countryside, often idealising nature as an alternative to urban society or courtly life.

Common features:

* Shepherds and rural settings

* Nature and simplicity

* Love, loss, and reflection

* Contrast between rural and urban life

* Sometimes political or social criticism beneath the idealised surface

📖 Famous examples:

* Edmund Spenser, The Shepheardes Calender

* Christopher Marlowe, “The Passionate Shepherd to His Love”

* John Milton, Lycidas

* Percy Bysshe Shelley, Adonais

🎯 UGC NET tip: Pastoral is a genre or mode, not one fixed verse form. A pastoral poem can also be an elegy, as in Lycidas.

## 15. 🌼 Idyll

Definition: An idyll is a short literary work, often a poem, that depicts a peaceful, picturesque, or idealised scene of rural life, nature, or human relationships.

It may also describe a brief period of happiness or an idealised experience.

📖 Famous examples:

* Theocritus, Idylls

* Alfred, Lord Tennyson, Idylls of the King

* Matthew Arnold, “The Scholar-Gipsy” is sometimes discussed in relation to idealised pastoral settings, although it is not simply a conventional idyll.

🎯 UGC NET tip: Theocritus is important in the history of Greek pastoral poetry. Tennyson's Idylls of the King is a series of narrative poems based on Arthurian legend, so the term idyll does not always mean a short, peaceful rural poem.

## ✍️ Five distinctions to memorise

1. Blank verse vs free verse: Blank verse is metrically regular and unrhymed; free verse has no prescribed regular metre or rhyme scheme.

2. Elegy vs ode: An elegy centres on mourning or loss; an ode centres on praise or sustained reflection.

3. Epic vs ballad: An epic is generally an extended heroic narrative; a ballad is generally a shorter, song-like narrative.

4. Pastoral vs idyll: Pastoral is a broader genre or mode of rural representation; an idyll is an idealised scene or literary composition.

5. Lyric vs dramatic monologue: A lyric foregrounds emotion or reflection; a dramatic monologue foregrounds a speaker whose character emerges through an address to a listener.
